## The August 17 outage, and the work ahead

- 原文链接: [The August 17 outage, and the work ahead](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/the-august-17-outage-and-the-work-ahead/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49378957)

GitHub had a big outage on August 17 that lasted almost 8 hours and stopped many services. This was their second big problem in August, and it affected developers and companies all over the world.

The outage happened because GitHub’s systems could not handle a new high level of traffic. One important part in their main data center failed to scale up, which caused other problems and stopped things like authentication, APIs, and Copilot from working. To fix it, GitHub teams had to reroute traffic and slowly bring services back online. Copilot was the slowest to recover because a bug caused too many retry requests, making things worse. The company explained that these outages were not because of bad code or config changes, but because their systems ran out of capacity as usage grew fast—monthly commits doubled in a few months.

Since then, GitHub added a lot more hardware: millions of CPU cores, lots of storage, and better networks. They also moved more of their service to Azure, which now runs over half of their load. They are working towards a new system that will let more people read data at once, starting with their biggest repositories. GitHub is also making their systems more reliable by testing better, rolling out updates more safely, and splitting up important services so that one failure doesn’t break everything. They are adding rules to stop retry storms and looking at alerts to catch weak spots during busy times. GitHub says they are serious about fixing these problems and wants users to trust them again.

In the comments, many people said they were upset because they depend on GitHub for their work. Some users were thankful for the honest explanation and the details about what went wrong and how GitHub is fixing things. Others wondered why GitHub waited until a big failure to fix these problems and said the company should have predicted more traffic. A few commenters talked about the risks of relying on just one platform and suggested that teams should always have backups or mirrors of their code. Some users felt moving more to Azure was a good idea, but others worried about too much dependence on Microsoft’s cloud. There was also talk about how hard it is to grow and scale systems for millions of users, and some people shared their own stories about outages at their companies. A few praised GitHub for being open, while others wanted more regular updates and better communication during outages. Overall, the community wants GitHub to be more reliable and to learn from these mistakes.

## I like 'em thick: an apology to my English teachers

- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49347543)

This article is about the idea of “thickness” in art and writing, and how the author changed his mind about the value of classic literature and art. He used to think great works were boring and only liked by “snobs,” but now sees that some art gives you more the deeper you look.

The author explains that “thick” art or writing is full of detail, history, and hidden meaning. It rewards people who pay attention and come back to it over time. He says that, as a student, no teacher explained this—he was just told to read and left alone, so he missed the treasure inside. He shares the story of seeing the painting “The Garden of Earthly Delights” by Bosch, which is full of strange and funny details, like music notes written on a man’s butt. At first, he thought this was just a fun fact, but after reading more, he learned that the music was probably meant as a joke or even a warning, not to be played. This shows how deep and surprising “thick” art can be.

He gives more examples, like how some books and paintings let you find new things each time you see them. He says “thick” art often asks the reader or viewer to do some work—like finding clues in a story or thinking about why the artist made certain choices. He compares this to cake mix that asks you to crack an egg, making you feel you’re part of the process. He also talks about how much time and effort real artists give, like a magician risking burns to eat fire, which makes the performance feel important.

The opposite of “thickness” is “slop” or “thin” art—books or movies that look real but have nothing deep inside. He warns that a lot of modern things, like self-help books or AI-generated art, can be like this: they look nice at first but fall apart when you look closer. He says that truly “thick” work often lasts for years or centuries, while “thin” work is quickly forgotten.

He worries that today, people pretend all art is equal, and “thickness” is less valued. He says this is why AI art might seem popular at first, but it doesn’t last or mean much to people. He argues that only things that have survived a long time usually have real value, which is why old stories and art are still important. He finishes by saying that making something “thick” is hard work, and you can’t skip the process by using AI or shortcuts.

In the Hacker News comments, many people agreed that school ruined classic books for them, because teachers didn’t help students see the value—they just forced them to read. Some said the best teachers showed how to explore deeper, while others said they only learned to enjoy classics later in life. Many liked the idea that great art needs time and effort from both the creator and the viewer.

Some commenters added that AI-created art is “thin” because it doesn’t have real effort or history behind it. Others said AI can sometimes surprise them, but it lacks the layers and “thickness” that comes from real people’s struggles and choices. A few thought the article was a bit harsh on new art or technology, and argued that “thickness” can show up in modern work, too.

Others shared their own stories of being bored by art museums until they found one piece that grabbed their attention and made them look closer. Some said the “crack the egg” idea was a good way to think about what makes something feel meaningful. There was also talk about how people want quick, easy entertainment now, so “thin” art is winning for the moment, but real “thickness” will always matter for those who want more.

A few users pointed out the problem with too much “elitism,” but most agreed that it’s good to have special things that take work to understand. Others joked about the “butt music” story and said it showed how deep and weird old art can be. Overall, the comments showed a lot of agreement that “thick” art is special, and that shortcuts like AI can’t really replace it.

## Show HN: Huzzah – a novel approach to coding with AI

- 原文链接: [Show HN: Huzzah – a novel approach to coding with AI](https://www.danielvaughn.dev/posts/huzzah/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49378768)

This post is about Huzzah, a new tool for coding with AI that uses a different method than current coding agents. The author explains that, after the excitement of using AI to write code wore off, he became tired of writing long, detailed English prompts, but also did not want to go back to manual coding.

He says current coding agents have some problems. First, there is no clear record of what the human wanted, because prompts are thrown away after use. Second, AI chats use step-by-step commands to change code, which wastes tokens and repeats information. Third, writing in normal English is slow and not always clear, since most language is not direct.

To fix this, he is building Huzzah. With Huzzah, you write prompts as pseudocode. These prompts are short, clear, and stay with the project. They are not just for one time use. For example, to make a “fizz buzz” program, you write a simple pseudocode file, save it, and Huzzah turns it into real code. If you want to change something, you just edit the pseudocode and save again. The tool uses the changes as prompts for the AI, so the code always matches what you wrote.

The author says this method has benefits. The pseudocode is easier to read and acts as documentation. You can write as much or as little as you want. It lets you design the code instead of just telling the AI what to do, and you could use the same pseudocode for different programming languages.

But there are problems too. This system might not work well for very big projects. It is better for new code than for old, messy code. If you do not know the topic well, using plain English might still be easier. Some things, like linking files together, could be hard. Features like code navigation are not ready yet.

In the comments, some people like the idea. They say writing pseudocode is natural for programmers, and having clear intent is helpful. Others point out challenges. They worry that the AI might not always understand the pseudocode, or that large teams may struggle to keep things in sync. Some users are excited to try Huzzah and like the focus on clear, simple prompts. A few mention that this could make onboarding new developers easier, since the pseudocode acts as documentation. Some wish there were more examples for bigger projects. Others say that, while this is interesting, it may not solve all problems with AI coding, especially for complex apps. A few also say that they still prefer traditional coding for full control, but are interested to see how Huzzah grows.

## AliExpress runs silent WebAudio fingerprinting that breaks Bluetooth multipoint

- 原文链接: [AliExpress runs silent WebAudio fingerprinting that breaks Bluetooth multipoint](https://blog.laserphile.com/2026/08/aliexpress-webpage-keeping-multipoint.html)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49372583)

AliExpress uses hidden scripts on its homepage that create silent audio processing in your browser. This causes problems for people using Bluetooth headphones with multipoint, stopping audio from their phone when AliExpress is open on the PC.

The writer noticed that opening AliExpress in Firefox or Chrome kept their Bluetooth headphones connected to the PC, blocking sound from their phone. Muting the browser or system did not help, and there was no visible audio or video playing. After checking the page, the writer found no normal media elements or playback calls. However, after a few seconds, the page created two invisible Web Audio contexts. These were made by scripts called collina.js and fireyejs.js, which seem to be part of Alibaba's security and anti-fraud tools.

These scripts use the Web Audio API to generate a silent sound (by setting the volume to zero) but still connect this to the system audio output. The browser treats this as live audio, keeping the audio path active. Besides Web Audio, these scripts also collect many other details from the browser, like graphics details, device features, plugins, and user interactions. All this data creates a strong browser fingerprint, which helps AliExpress track users and separate real users from bots or fraud.

AliExpress wants to fight fraud and bots, so this kind of fingerprinting helps them even when cookies are cleared. The problem is that this fingerprinting is done secretly, even when just browsing the homepage. It also causes real hardware problems, like breaking Bluetooth multipoint, and there’s no easy way to see or control it.

The writer blocks these scripts using uBlock Origin by adding two filter rules. This stops the hidden audio processing and lets their headphones work normally again. They warn that blocking these scripts might cause more CAPTCHAs, but normal browsing and shopping still work.

Hacker News commenters have many thoughts about this. Some say AliExpress is right to use anti-fraud tools, but most people dislike how hidden and aggressive the fingerprinting is. Many are surprised that silent audio processing can break hardware features like Bluetooth multipoint. Some suggest browser makers should give users more control over background audio activity, while others want better ways to stop fingerprinting. There are warnings that blocking scripts could make AliExpress harder to use, but people agree privacy is important. A few share tips on blocking similar scripts on other shopping sites. Others worry about how much tracking happens on the web today, even just for visiting a store's homepage. Some hope browsers will soon block or warn about hidden fingerprinting like this. Overall, people think this is sneaky and want more transparency from big sites.

## HTML Can Do That

- 原文链接: [HTML Can Do That](https://chrisburnell.com/html-can-do-that/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49362689)

This article talks about new features in HTML that let you do things that used to need JavaScript. The author shows how plain HTML can now create popovers, dialogs, accordions, lazy-loaded images, color pickers, autocomplete, and more.

You can use the `popover` attribute to make popups that open and close without JavaScript. The `<dialog>` element lets you build modal windows, and now you can open or close them in HTML, or still use JavaScript if you want. The grouped `<details>` elements let you make an accordion, so when you open one, the other closes—again, all in HTML. The `command` and `commandfor` attributes give you new ways to control popovers and dialogs by linking buttons to actions, with no scripts needed. The `loading="lazy"` attribute on images delays loading until the picture is almost on the screen, so your page loads faster without extra code. There is also `hidden="until-found"` for hiding content until you click a link or search for it, and the browser shows it.

HTML now gives you built-in color pickers, range sliders, and date pickers. These are easy to use, but the article warns they don’t always look the same on every browser, and sometimes they’re not easy to use for everyone. The `<datalist>` tag creates autocomplete for text fields, but support is not perfect yet. For all these features, the author says you should always check if they work well for everyone, especially people using screen readers or other assistive tools.

In the comments, many people are excited to see how much HTML can do now. Some say this makes web development simpler, faster, and easier to maintain. They like not needing so much JavaScript. Others are a bit cautious—some features don’t work in older browsers, or aren’t accessible for all users. A few mention that browser support is still uneven, especially for things like `popover` and `datalist`. Some say they tried these features and found small bugs or problems with keyboard navigation. Others wish browser makers would fix these issues soon, so everyone can use the new HTML tools. A few developers suggest that for production websites, it’s still safest to check fallbacks or add a bit of JavaScript for better support. But overall, most commenters are hopeful and happy to see HTML growing, and want to use these features when they can.

## Malicious Rust crate Arrayref runs a build-time payload

- 原文链接: [Malicious Rust crate Arrayref runs a build-time payload](https://safedep.io/arrayref-proc-macro1-rust-build-time-malware/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49374269)

A popular Rust package called `arrayref` was hacked and a bad version was uploaded to crates.io. In version 0.3.10, it added a dependency on a fake crate named `proc-macro1`, which secretly downloaded and ran a program when anyone built their project.

The real maintainer’s account was taken over, and both the code and GitHub page disappeared. The fake `proc-macro1` was made to look like a normal Rust library, copying code from the real `proc-macro2` crate. But it hid a build script that took a secret server address, downloaded a file, and ran it on your computer—just by compiling any Rust project that depended on it.

The attack worked because `arrayref` is used in many other Rust projects, sometimes very deep in their dependency lists. The hacker made older safe versions “yanked” (unusable), so Cargo warned people to upgrade to the only available version, which was actually the bad one. This trick led more people to use the hack without knowing.

The malicious code only needed a single line added to the list of dependencies. Even though `arrayref` itself had no code that used `proc-macro1`, Cargo still downloaded and built it. The build script in `proc-macro1` put together the server address using base64, hiding it from easy searches, and fetched the right kind of malware for your operating system. On Unix, it wrote and ran a hidden file in `/tmp`. On Windows, it used PowerShell and VBScript to run in the background without being noticed. The script ignored all security checks for downloading files, making it easy for the attacker to connect.

This hack was dangerous because `arrayref` had about 245 million downloads. Many Rust GUI and graphics projects (like those using `egui`, `eframe`, or `iced`) were likely affected since they depended on it indirectly.

In the Hacker News comments, many developers were very worried. Some said this proves the risk of using open source code without checking every dependency. Others pointed out that the Rust package system, like others (npm, PyPI), is not safe from these types of attacks. People discussed how easy it is to miss a malicious build script, since it can hide in an unused dependency.

Some users argued that package managers should never run code at build time, or at least give a clear warning. Others wondered if crates.io could require more checks or two-factor authentication for popular packages to prevent account takeovers. Some people said the attack was clever, especially the trick of yanking old versions to push users to the hacked one.

A few developers said they would now review their own dependencies more carefully, and some asked for better tools to detect these problems. Others felt it’s impossible to check everything and that the software ecosystem needs bigger changes. Many agreed that open source security is now a very serious problem for everyone, not just big companies.

## Linux 7.2

- 原文链接: [Linux 7.2](https://www.igalia.com/2026/08/19/Linux-72-Released.html)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49376265)

Linux 7.2 is a new release of the Linux kernel, and it was a very busy development cycle with many changes and fixes. Some of the big improvements are cache-aware scheduling, better memory handling with MGLRU, new ideas for scheduling with sched_ext, and better support for huge memory pages. Igalia, a company that works on Linux, made many contributions this cycle.

One highlight is a new fair scheduler for the GPU, which helps when many programs share the GPU. However, a last-minute bug means it is not the default yet, but you can turn it on if you want to try it. The team also made it easier to debug problems with custom schedulers, especially on computers with lots of CPU cores. For Raspberry Pi 4 and 5, there is now runtime power management for the GPU, so the GPU uses less power when it is not busy. They also fixed old bugs that caused crashes for RetroPie users on Raspberry Pi 3.

Another fix was for a 14-year-old bug in the futex system call, which is important for programs that need to work together safely. There were other bugfixes too, like one for a USB driver that sometimes caused problems when connecting or disconnecting a device, and some improvements in how memory is checked during computer startup. Some progress was also made toward full HDMI 2.1 support, which will help with modern displays.

In the comments, some people are happy about the new power management for Raspberry Pi, as it can save energy and help make devices last longer. Others are interested in the new scheduler features, saying it is great to see more work on making Linux better for different kinds of computers. A few users wish the fair GPU scheduler was turned on by default, but they understand why it is not yet. Some developers talk about the long-standing futex bug and are glad it is finally fixed, even if it took many years.

There are also comments about how big the Linux kernel has become, with so many changes each release. Some users think it is a good sign that Linux is always getting better, while others worry about possible new bugs or the need for more testing. A few Raspberry Pi fans share their experiences with crashes and look forward to seeing if the new fixes help them. Others discuss the importance of better HDMI support, especially for people using Linux with new monitors or TVs.

Some developers thank Igalia for their work and for sharing so much detail about what changed. There are questions about when the fair GPU scheduler will be ready for everyone. A few people also talk about the value of good documentation and testing in the Linux community. Overall, the release is seen as another strong step forward for Linux, with both big new features and important bugfixes.
