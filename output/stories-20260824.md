## Xiaomi: New CPU matches Apple cores single threaded, much faster multithreaded

- 原文链接: [Xiaomi: New CPU matches Apple cores single threaded, much faster multithreaded](https://twitter.com/lemire/status/2091894299289874926)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49420873)

Xiaomi just released a new CPU for their phones, and it’s as fast as Apple’s best chip for single tasks and even faster when doing many things at once. The chip, called Xring O3, may not be easy to find in stores yet, and Apple might soon release something even better, but this chip shows some big changes in phone technology. The new Xiaomi CPU comes with a huge amount of cache memory—44 MB, which is more than most laptop CPUs, even many Intel laptops. Its most powerful core is called C1-Ultra, which supports SME2 (for AI work) and SVE2 (for working on lots of data at once). The core is very wide, with 21 execution ports, and 6 of those can handle SIMD, or “do lots of similar math at the same time.” This is more than what Intel or AMD chips usually have. For example, AMD Zen 5 can do more in one go (4x512-bit), but Xiaomi’s 6x128-bit is the best for ARM chips right now. The trend is clear: more parts in the CPU can do math at the same time, and there’s more cache memory. All these improvements help the chip do many tasks quickly. In a Geekbench test, the chip scored 3,945 for single-core and 15,221 for multi-core—very high numbers for a phone.

People in the comments are both excited and a bit unsure. Some think the chip sounds amazing, with new tech and high scores, but they want to see real proof. One person says the numbers look a bit “too good,” since another chip with a C1-Ultra core (Tensor G6) only gets 2,700 for single-core. Others are excited about the 21 execution ports and SVE2 support, saying it could be very fast for data tasks. A few worry that these numbers might not be real or could change in real phones. Some compare it to Apple and wonder how long Xiaomi will keep this lead, since Apple updates their chips often. Others are just impressed by how much cache and parallel math the new chip can do, saying it shows how phone CPUs are getting more like desktop chips. Overall, people are interested but want to see more real-world tests.

## One corner of China’s internet is insisting that the Tang Dynasty never existed

- 原文链接: [One corner of China’s internet is insisting that the Tang Dynasty never existed](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/19/style/china-tang-dynasty-never-existed-hoax-intl-hnk)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49425819)

A group online in China has started saying that the famous Tang Dynasty never happened. The Tang Dynasty was a big part of Chinese history, known for culture, trade, and power, and is taught in schools and shown on TV.

The rumor started when a history influencer named Qiao Yu posted a video using strange astrology to argue the Tang Dynasty was fake. Some people then called the tourist office in Xi’an, the old Tang capital, and asked them to close, saying the Tang never existed. The idea spread fast on Chinese social media, with the hashtag "the Tang dynasty doesn't exist" getting millions of views. 

It’s hard to know why people are spreading this idea. Some nationalists in China don’t like the Tang because its first emperor was not Han Chinese, while the next dynasty, the Song, was Han. Qiao Yu’s claim is that the Song dynasty began earlier, erasing the Tang from history. Most experts say this is nonsense—Harvard professor William Kirby says the Tang dynasty is a proven fact, and was important not just for China but for the whole region.

As more people talked about the rumor online, Chinese state media stepped in. Newspapers and TV said the claim was wrong and dangerous, warning that doubting history could hurt people’s confidence in China’s culture. The government calls these rumors “historical nihilism” and punishes people for questioning official history. President Xi Jinping often talks about China’s long history, including the glory of the Tang, to promote national pride.

Some historians say looking back at history is a way for people in China to talk about today’s problems—sometimes as a way to safely criticize the government or society. A young person in Beijing told CNN that doubting the Tang is a kind of rebellion against older people and experts, making young people feel special or smarter.

Soon, the government blocked Qiao Yu’s accounts and removed all related videos. Now, anyone who searches about the Tang dynasty on Chinese social media mostly sees posts saying the rumor is false.

In the Hacker News comments, many users call the hoax silly and compare it to people denying major events in their own countries. Some think it shows how quickly fake news spreads online, even when the facts are clear. Others say it’s interesting how history can be used to push political ideas or to rebel. A few point out that every country has strange conspiracy theories and that this is not just a Chinese problem. Several comments discuss how governments try to control history to keep power. Some people feel sorry for Chinese students who may now be confused about their own history. A few users joke about what other “obvious” things people might try to deny next. Others warn that blocking people only makes rumors more interesting to some, instead of stopping them. Finally, some say the story is funny, but also a little sad, because it shows how easy it is for people to doubt even the most basic facts.

## MS Paint and Photos inivisibly watermark even locally generated output with GUID

- 原文链接: [MS Paint and Photos inivisibly watermark even locally generated output with GUID](https://xusheng.dev/posts/reversing/mspaint_invisible_watermark/main/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49421158)

Microsoft Paint and Photos now add an invisible watermark to images you create with their AI tools, even if you generate them on your own computer. This watermark is a special code called a GUID, given by Microsoft’s server after your image prompt is checked for safety.

The article explains how Paint and Photos use local AI models to make images, but before making the image, they always send your prompt to Microsoft’s servers for moderation. The server checks your prompt and sends back a GUID. This GUID is hidden inside the pixels of your new image by a software tool called Watermarker.dll. Even if you turn off visible watermarks, this hidden one is always added. If the watermark cannot be added, Paint will not give you the image at all.

The watermark is 16 bytes long and is added using a special method that changes many pixels in a way you cannot see. The GUID links your image to the prompt you used. Microsoft also puts extra information in the image file using the C2PA standard, which states the image was made by AI and includes the same GUID.

Even when image generation is local, you still need an internet connection and a Microsoft account, because the prompt must be checked online and the watermark comes from the server. You can only save AI images in formats that keep this extra data, like PNG, JPEG, GIF, or Microsoft’s own .paint files.

The article also found that the Photos app does the same thing. If watermarking fails in Photos, you still get the image, but not in Paint.

Microsoft says in its help pages that it filters prompts and adds C2PA data to AI images. But it does not clearly say that every AI image includes a unique, hidden identifier linked to your prompt and that this comes from Microsoft’s servers. This could have privacy and user rights concerns.

In the Hacker News comments, some users are surprised and worried about privacy. They point out that people might not expect a locally-made image to get a remote-unique ID, especially when they are not using the cloud. Others say this is part of following new AI rules, like those in the EU, which require clear proof that images are AI-made. Some developers feel this is not real “local” generation if the prompt always goes online.

A few commenters say watermarking is good for stopping abuse, like spreading fake images. But others think it could be misused to track users or limit their freedom. Some note that if you really want to avoid this, you can use open tools like Stable Diffusion directly, which do not add these watermarks. There are also worries about whether this system could be bypassed and if that would be a security risk.

Some users wonder if Microsoft should be clearer in their disclosures and if users should be able to turn this off. Others think most people will not care, but power users should know what is happening with their data and images. Some ask if this could hurt trust in Microsoft’s AI tools. Overall, the discussion is a mix of technical curiosity, privacy worries, and debate on the role of AI labeling.

## Oceans hit highest temperature on record

- 原文链接: [Oceans hit highest temperature on record](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c62m4gpnp78o)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49424606)

The article says that the world’s oceans are now hotter than ever before, reaching an average temperature of 21.1°C. This new record is linked to both human-caused climate change and the growing El Niño weather event. Scientists explain that oceans usually get hottest in March or April, but this record happened in August, which is unusual. El Niño is a natural event that makes ocean water warmer, but this time it’s adding to heat already caused by people burning fossil fuels. Most of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases goes into the oceans—over 90%. 

Warmer oceans can cause many problems. They make storms stronger, raise sea levels, and damage sea life like coral reefs. Some places, such as the western English Channel, have had marine heatwaves for more than three years, which has never happened before. Scientists are worried because El Niño is still getting stronger and could make temperatures go even higher by the end of the year. They also note that parts of the ocean far from the Pacific are much warmer than usual. This shows that climate change is affecting seas all over the world, not just in one area. Warmer sea water takes up more space, which means more flooding along coasts. It also brings more extreme weather and puts pressure on people and animals that depend on the ocean.

Hacker News users share different thoughts about the news. Some are shocked and worried about the rapid changes, saying this is proof that climate change is getting worse. A few people ask if the data is correct or if small errors could change the result, but most agree the trend is real. Others discuss how warmer oceans will make storms, floods, and fires more common, and some users point out it could hurt fishing and tourism jobs. There are comments about the need to reduce fossil fuel use and develop cleaner energy. Some users feel hopeless, thinking it is too late to stop big changes, while others say even small actions to cut pollution can help. A few share ideas for cooling the oceans, but most agree solutions will be hard and need global effort. Overall, the comment section shows a mix of worry, debate about the science, and calls for action.

## IPFS Maintainers Winding Down

- 原文链接: [IPFS Maintainers Winding Down](https://ipshipyard.com/blog/2026-the-end-of-ipfs-at-shipyard/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49421489)

Shipyard, a team working on the IPFS project, announced they are stopping their work because Protocol Labs will not fund them anymore. Their last day working on IPFS will be September 30, 2026.

Shipyard was important to IPFS. They made websites and downloads easier to verify and use in browsers. They made the IPFS gateway faster and cheaper to run. They worked on new ways to use IPFS with HTTP, making it simpler for developers. They also helped maintain popular IPFS tools and public services.

Shipyard wanted to keep improving IPFS. Their goals included making IPFS easier to use with HTTP, better content routing, support for bigger files, and more privacy features. Now, they will not be able to finish these projects.

Many things will change. Projects like Kubo, Helia, IPFS Desktop, and others will lose their main maintainers. Shipyard will stop helping with other important code. They will also stop running public services like ipfs.io, dweb.link, and Wikipedia-on-IPFS. Protocol Labs will decide what happens to these services next.

Shipyard says they will help with the transition until the end of September. They thank the community and hope their work will help future projects.

In the comments, some people are sad and worried. They say this is a big loss for the IPFS community and for people who use these services. Others wonder if IPFS has problems with funding or if it is too hard to keep running these systems. Some are hopeful that new people or groups will step in to help. A few commenters discuss technical issues with IPFS, like slow speeds, and hope someone will fix them. Some say this shows the risk of depending on one company or small group for important open-source tools. Others thank Shipyard for their hard work and say they will miss them. One person asks if Protocol Labs will keep supporting IPFS at all. Another says maybe this is a chance for the community to step up and take over some projects. Some people are not surprised, saying it is normal for open-source projects to change maintainers. Overall, people feel sad but also want to see what happens next.

## How Europe is killing makers and micro-entrepreneurs

- 原文链接: [How Europe is killing makers and micro-entrepreneurs](https://lectronz.com/u/lectronz/articles/how-europe-is-killing-makers-and-micro-entrepreneurs)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49419237)

This article talks about new packaging rules in the European Union that are making it very hard for small makers and micro-businesses to sell products across EU countries. The author runs Lectronz, a small marketplace for DIY electronics, and explains how these rules hurt people who just want to share what they make.

The EU wants to reduce packaging waste, which is a good idea. They made new rules (called EPR and PPWR) that say anyone selling products must pay for the recycling of their packaging in every country they sell to. But the way the rules work is very complicated. Each small seller has to register and pay fees in every EU country where they have customers. This is easy for big companies, but for people who only sell a few things a year, the cost is huge. For example, an engineer selling just ten boards to four countries would have to pay over €1,000 in fees every year, even if their packaging waste is very small. If you want to sell to all 27 EU countries, you would have to sell thousands of products just to break even. Most makers, artists, and small business owners can’t afford this.

The article says this is a problem because new ideas often come from small people trying things, not big companies. Many sellers on Lectronz only sell a few items a year just to test ideas. These rules could stop them from selling at all and kill innovation. The author also says that the rules could hurt Lectronz itself, since if sellers leave, the platform cannot survive.

To fix this, the author suggests three solutions: first, to make a rule that small sellers with low sales and waste do not have to pay or register; second, to make a single EU website where small sellers can pay and register for all countries at once; third, to let platforms like Lectronz handle the rules for all their sellers as one group, not as many small businesses. The author says the EU should use easy online systems, not slow and difficult paperwork.

The article warns that if nothing changes, small makers may stop selling in the EU and only ship to places like the US, which is bad for the EU economy and for people who want to buy unique products. The author asks readers to sign a petition and write feedback to the European Commission, because these rules affect not just electronics, but also artists, craftspeople, and food producers.

In the comments, many people agree that the rules are too hard for small businesses. Some say the EU always makes things more complex than needed and does not think about small sellers. Others point out that big companies can handle these rules easily, so it helps them but hurts small ones. A few commenters say the idea of reducing waste is important, but the way the rules work is not fair. Some suggest that a simple, EU-wide system would be much better for everyone. One person says this is another example of how the EU sometimes hurts its own people with too much regulation.

Some commenters mention that similar rules are starting in other places, like the US, and hope this does not become normal everywhere. Others say that small businesses will just stop selling to the EU, which will make it harder for buyers to find interesting products. A few worry that these rules may not even help the environment much, since big companies can still create a lot of waste. Some suggest that lobbying or more public feedback could help change the rules. Others are sad that this might kill small creativity and say it shows how hard it is to start something new in Europe. Overall, most agree that something needs to change for small makers to survive.

## NIST is selling Peanut Butter for $415.33 per jar

- 原文链接: [NIST is selling Peanut Butter for $415.33 per jar](https://shop.nist.gov/ccrz__ProductDetails?sku=2387)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49426118)

NIST, a U.S. government agency, is selling peanut butter for over $1,200 for three jars, which is about $415 for each jar. This sounds strange because peanut butter in a store usually costs only a few dollars.

But this is not normal peanut butter. NIST sells it as a Standard Reference Material (SRM). Scientists use SRMs to check their lab equipment and test methods. The peanut butter is measured very carefully to know exactly what is inside, like the amount of fats, proteins, vitamins, and any metals or chemicals. Labs use it to make sure their tests are correct and their reports are true. The jars are kept frozen and shipped on dry ice. The price also pays for the testing, special packing, and paperwork that comes with it. NIST has other food SRMs too, like fish, tea, and cereal, and all of them are expensive for the same reason.

In the top comments, many people laugh about the price and joke about how it must taste amazing or make very fancy sandwiches. Some people explain why it costs so much, saying that making reference materials takes a lot of work and careful science. Others point out that labs, not regular people, buy these to check their machines, so the price is not crazy for them. A few say the cost is still high, but it is worth it for labs that need exact results. One person remembers using NIST peanut butter in school for a science class. Some wonder if the government makes money on this, but others say NIST just covers its costs. There are also comments comparing the peanut butter price to other strange, expensive lab items. Overall, people agree this is not for lunch—it’s for science.

## Jabber/XMPP: 25 Years of Digital Independence

- 原文链接: [Jabber/XMPP: 25 Years of Digital Independence](https://gultsch.de/posts/25-years-of-digital-independence/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49421536)

This article talks about why owning our digital communication tools is important, using Jabber/XMPP as a good example. The writer explains that digital services, like messaging apps, should be viewed as basic infrastructure, just like roads or water systems.

Many people in Europe did not think about who controls their digital tools, often trusting big American tech companies. The author says this is not wise, and we should have real ownership and control, not just switch to European companies doing the same thing. Open standards, like XMPP, help people switch tools or providers when needed, instead of being stuck with one company. Open-source software is good, but it is not enough if you cannot move your data or keep chatting when a company shuts down. True freedom comes from systems that can work together (called interoperability), so anyone can host their own server if they want, or join a group one.

The article explains how XMPP is a real open standard. It is managed by a group (the XSF) where anyone can suggest changes, and many different apps and servers use it. This helps avoid being locked into a single company. Other tools, like Matrix, are open-source but are still controlled by one company, making it hard for outsiders to help improve things.

Over the years, XMPP has changed and added new features by using extensions (called XEPs). Some changes were slow, but today XMPP apps can do almost everything modern messengers can: emoji, read receipts, end-to-end encryption, and more. The community is still active, adding things like image galleries and better login options. There are different XMPP apps for different needs, so people can choose what works best for them.

In the comments, some people say XMPP is still too hard for most people to use. They mention problems like confusing setup, server choices, and not enough polish compared to apps like WhatsApp or Signal. Others reply that things have improved a lot, with apps like Conversations (Android) and Dino (Linux) being easy to use now. A few people wish XMPP was more popular, but think it lost ground when mobile apps became common and XMPP was slow to adapt.

Some users say that even if XMPP is open and free, most people just want something simple and do not care about open standards. Others think that governments and organizations should use XMPP to avoid being locked in by big tech. There are also comments warning that open standards are only useful if enough people use them and keep them alive. Some wish more new features would come faster, but others like the slow, careful pace because it keeps things stable and safe. Overall, the comments show hope that XMPP can still play a big role in the future of messaging.
