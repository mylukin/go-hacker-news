We start with a new study about semaglutide, a drug mainly used for diabetes and weight loss. Researchers found that people taking semaglutide had a lower chance of getting dementia compared to those not taking the drug. The drug helps control blood sugar and appetite, but scientists wanted to know if it could also protect the brain. They used medical records and computer models to predict dementia risk and saw lower numbers for semaglutide users, especially those with diabetes, though even people without diabetes saw some benefit. However, the study does not prove the drug prevents dementia—it only shows a possible link. The authors warn that computer models can make mistakes, and real world tests are needed. In the comments, some users are hopeful, calling the result exciting, while others are more careful. They remind us that a lower predicted risk is not the same as proof, and computer models are not always right. Some worry about cost and side effects if semaglutide is used more widely. Others share stories of friends or family using the drug and wonder if it could help their brains too. Many point out that drugs often have extra effects, good or bad, and that lifestyle changes like eating better and exercising still matter for brain health. There’s excitement to see more research connecting diabetes and brain health, with hope for better treatments in the future.

Next, we look at an article about creativity and solitude. The writer explains that new ideas are often born when people are alone, away from group pressure. They use stories from famous people like mathematician Alexander Grothendieck and film director Ingmar Bergman. In the past, many thought that working together in coworking spaces would help startups, but this often did not work well. Sam Altman from Y Combinator said that great ideas are fragile and can be killed by group pressure, especially when they are new and strange. The article says solitude is not just being alone in a room, but having a mind free from others’ opinions. Grothendieck worked alone for years and was patient with confusion, which helped him find new questions. Other creative people, like Bergman, used notebooks and daily habits to protect early ideas. The article gives tips: wait before sharing, think of your work as serving something higher, work with people who push you to be original, or create fast to avoid self-censorship. In the comments, some agree that solitude is key to creativity, while others say teamwork is also needed to polish ideas. Some warn that working alone can be risky or lonely, and that business often rewards safe, group-approved ideas. There are stories about having good ideas while walking or letting the mind wander. Some wish schools and offices would give more space for thinking alone. Others warn that too much time alone, without feedback, can lead to going in circles.

Now, let’s talk about AI and math. A new article explains that AI is not smarter than people at math, but it has a much bigger working memory. AI can remember every step and rule, just like having endless scratch paper. Humans forget steps or lose track, which makes big math problems hard. Studies show that children with better working memory do better at math. AI’s big advantage is being able to hold dozens or hundreds of facts at once, which is very useful for math, since everything can be written out and checked. But AI is not like Einstein, who made creative leaps—it is more like a very fast, organized worker. In the comments, many agree that AI’s memory and speed are its strengths, not creativity. Some say it is unfair to compare AI with humans working without notes. Others say that good mathematicians also use tools, so maybe the gap is not so big. Some point out that AI still makes silly mistakes or misses the main idea. There are worries that AI doing all the “bookkeeping” could make people lazy. Some enjoy the comparison of AI to von Neumann, a fast, organized thinker, while creative leaps still belong to humans. Many agree that the real skill for humans in the future will be asking the right questions and seeing things in a new way.

In health news, there’s a new at-home test called LymeAlert that lets people check if a tick that bit them carries Lyme disease. Tick bites and Lyme disease are rising in the US, and quick treatment is important. The test is simple: you crush the tick, add a liquid, and use a test strip to see if the tick had Lyme bacteria. There is also an app to read results and connect you to a doctor. This is faster than sending ticks to a lab, and blood tests often miss early cases. Experts say the test is helpful but not perfect—a positive tick does not mean you will get sick, and only a small percent of bites lead to Lyme disease. In the comments, some users are excited about the test and say it could save time and worry. Others point out the limits: a positive does not guarantee infection, and a negative might give false comfort. Some worry about overusing antibiotics, while others think it is a good tool if people understand it is not perfect. There are debates about its $50 cost, and many agree that public awareness and education about ticks are still very important.

Switching to hardware, there’s a critical article about the RISC-V CPU architecture. The author says RISC-V tries to be good for everything, but this is impossible. Small devices need fast interrupts and small code, but RISC-V is slower than ARM Cortex-M0. For big servers, the instruction design causes problems, like making it hard to decode instructions fast. Many features in RISC-V are optional, so different chips support different instructions, making it hard to write software that works everywhere. Even important things like multiplication or interrupts are not always present. The way instructions are encoded is also confusing. To fix this, the RISC-V group made “profiles” for different uses, but most boards do not follow them, which may break software in the future. The author thinks RISC-V will do well for cheap devices, not because it is good, but because it is free. For high-performance uses, ARM and x86 are still better. In the comments, many agree that RISC-V’s optional features make things messy. Some say the base set is too small, while others defend the open nature and think things will improve. Some worry about software compatibility and RISC-V’s reputation, while others hope the market will choose better defaults.

Most were excited about using AI to speed up experiments, but agreed that humans still need to review the results.

We also remember Bede Liu, who died at age 91. He was a key figure in digital signal processing, or DSP, and a professor at Princeton. Liu’s work made it possible to switch from analog to digital systems, which is why we can now stream music and video, use smartphones, and make video calls. He wrote a well-known textbook, published over 250 papers, and held 12 patents. He was also a mentor to many students who went on to become tech leaders. Later, he worked on protecting digital media from copying, and helped develop digital watermarks. In the comments, many users share respect for Liu’s impact, saying his work is “invisible” but powers much of today’s tech. Some talk about studying his textbook or using his research. Others say more people should know about the history of DSP and the people behind it. There are technical comments about his patents and how his ideas made hardware more efficient. Many remember him as friendly and encouraging.

In health news, another study says that fat around your stomach is a better sign of heart disease risk than BMI. The study looked at over 260,000 people for 20 years, checking their waist size and waist-to-hip ratio. Many people with a normal BMI still had a lot of belly fat and higher heart risk, while some with high BMI had less belly fat and lower risk. The study found that belly fat, called visceral fat, is worse for your heart than fat under your skin. Doctors now say that just looking at BMI is not enough. In the comments, people agree that BMI can be misleading, especially for people with more muscle. Some say measuring belly fat is practical and should be used more, while others share personal stories about their BMI and health. Some worry that focusing on belly fat might make people feel bad about their bodies, and others say that lifestyle still matters too, not just the numbers.

Finally, we have a strange story about “ghost characters” in a Japanese character standard from 1978. These were characters with no clear meaning or source, and even experts didn’t know where they came from. Some were created by mistakes, like when two papers were pasted together and a new line was formed by accident. Others came from misreading similar-looking characters. Despite being errors, these characters were added to the official standard and later to Unicode, so now they exist on almost every computer, even though no one uses them. In the comments, people were surprised and amused that simple mistakes could become part of global computer standards forever. Some saw it as a warning about how errors can spread in big systems, while others liked the idea of “mystery” characters hiding in their computers. Some developers said this is why good documentation and careful standards are important. Others shared similar stories from other languages, and a few joked about these ghosts as digital “Easter eggs.”
