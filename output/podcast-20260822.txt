Hello everyone, this is the 2026-08-22 episode of Hacker News Daily Podcast. Today, we bring you stories from the world of tech, programming, and digital life, with some unique personal experiences along the way.

First, let’s talk about a big change coming to macOS. Apple is removing the old ‘hdiutil’ command-line tool for managing disk images in the next version, macOS 27 Golden Gate. From now on, users are told to use ‘diskutil image’ instead. While most features from ‘hdiutil’ still exist under new names, some useful options are gone, like ‘-puppetstrings’ and special ways to handle folders. The article’s writer tested both tools by backing up a home folder. ‘hdiutil’ took about 110 seconds and required admin rights, giving clear errors and asking for a password. The new ‘diskutil image’ was faster—about 40 seconds—and the disk image was smaller, but it failed at first with a simple “Operation not permitted” message and did not explain why. It also skipped the Trash folder by default, as if the ‘scrub’ option was always on. The writer thinks ‘diskutil’ needs better error messages, clearer permission handling, and more control over scrubbing. He is also frustrated that Apple is removing ‘hdiutil’ when the features could be in both tools, and worries about broken scripts and old apps. Many commenters agree, sharing annoyance about losing old tools and missing features. Some note the speed of ‘diskutil’, but most want better documentation and helpful migration guides. There are also worries about Apple’s slow bug fixes and confusing support, with some hoping third-party tools will help, and others planning to delay upgrading their systems.

Now, a story from Moxie Marlinspike about moving to Pittsburgh and learning how hard winter can be, especially in an old, broken house. Around 2006, Moxie moved from the west coast, thinking winter would be fun, but soon found himself freezing in a house with no heat or working utilities. He and friends worked to fix things, using whatever materials they could find. When they took out an old bathtub and left it outside, it disappeared overnight—taken by local scrappers. They later put out another bathtub, and two men, Wade and Ron, arrived to collect it and even offered to help remove a furnace from the basement. The story turns funny and a bit scary as the group works together, with Wade feeling trapped and Ron grabbing an axe to get the furnace out. Moxie later learned these men worked very hard for little money, as steel paid only four cents a pound. In the comments, readers praised the writing and shared their own stories about scrappers and fixing up homes. Some liked the real, personal style, saying it reminded them of old blogs. Others discussed the hard work done by scrappers and the unexpected lessons you learn from moving to a new city.

Next, we have Gary’s story from the UK, showing how switching server software can change your life. Gary was a network admin whose company ran important servers on Windows, which often crashed and ruined his family time. After one crash during a trip with his daughter, Gary asked to try NetBSD on two servers. The NetBSD servers stayed up when the Windows ones failed, so his boss allowed more to be added. Soon, the whole network—serving thousands of users—ran on NetBSD, and things became stable. Gary and his team had fewer emergencies, more chances to learn, and more time with family. He thanked NetBSD and its developers for making life better. Many commenters praised the reliability of BSD systems and agreed that simple, stable software is key for work-life balance. Some shared their own success stories with BSD, while others noted it is important to use the right tool for your own job. While some wondered if NetBSD is still a good choice today, most agreed that stable systems and happy admins will always be important.

Moving on, there’s an article about why local LLMs—large language models run on your own computer—often seem less smart than you expect. Even if you download a famous LLM, it might perform worse than demos online. The main reason is that hardware, drivers, and settings differ for everyone. Small differences in GPUs or the math used can cause big changes in results. Using the wrong temperature or top-p settings can make the model stuck or repeat itself. The article shows that changing even small things—like number formats or attention backends—can lead to mistakes, especially with long inputs or heavy quantization. Many readers agree, sharing stories of local LLMs acting strange or making errors. Some say people expect too much from small or quantized models, while others want better documentation and standard tests. There are also calls for better ways to set the right parameters. Most agree that running LLMs locally is tricky, and hope for better guides and tools in the future.

Next, a lighthearted look at a naming trend in tech companies, especially those working with AI: calling themselves a number plus “Labs,” like ElevenLabs, TwelveLabs, and even up to 99Labs. The writer noticed this pattern, then searched for more examples and found many. The rules are simple: the name must have a number and “Labs,” and the company should be active online. Some numbers are more popular than others, and some companies have fun retro websites, like SeventyOneLab, which looks like it belongs in the early 2000s. In the comments, people find the trend funny, saying the names blur together and sound a bit lazy. Some think “Labs” makes a company sound modern or scientific, and adding a number is just for style or memorability. Others wonder about the meaning behind the numbers, or joke about starting their own “number+Labs” company. While some think the trend will fade, others enjoy the playful side, especially when sites have creative, retro designs.

Switching gears, let’s talk about Hister, a tool for building your own private search engine for web pages and files you choose. Hister is free software, runs on your own machine or server, and focuses on privacy—no tracking, and your data stays with you. It can index full content, not just titles, and offers previews in search results. You can add content through a browser extension, folder watching, importing history, or crawling websites. Hister works for both individuals and teams, supports SQLite and PostgreSQL, and is easy to run with Docker or Nix. It has version tracking, sensitive content checks, and supports many languages and file types. People in the comments like the privacy and control, though some point out that setting up your own server might be too hard for non-technical users. Others compare Hister to tools like Docspell or Recoll, and discuss the pros and cons. Many are excited about the open source license, while some worry about long-term support. Overall, Hister fills a gap for people wanting full control over their digital knowledge, but may not be for everyone.

Now, on to Racket, a language from the Lisp family, known for clear, regular rules and lots of parentheses. Racket is easy to read once you get used to its style, and it supports functional programming, which helps make programs safer and easier to test. Racket is popular for teaching, with good tools and books, and lets users build their own languages. It has strong error messages and a helpful package manager. Some commenters love Racket for learning and experimenting, saying the community is friendly and the documentation is good. Others find it slow for big projects and say the many parentheses are hard at first. A few warn that Racket is not common in jobs, but many agree it helps you think differently about programming and is a good language to try.

In another story, a student in Texas found and reported an AI-driven brute-force attack on a school computer system. The student noticed strange behavior in code during a project, realized an AI was trying many passwords quickly, and reported it to teachers and IT staff. The school found signs of attack, stopped it, and improved security. The article shows how AI can make hacking easier, and how important it is for people to be alert and report problems. In the comments, people were surprised by AI being used for hacking and agreed that better security and education are needed. Some say passwords alone are not enough, and call for two-factor authentication. Many praised the student for being responsible and hope more people will report suspicious activity.

It mixes old and new content: vintage magazine scans, electronics puzzles, history, and lessons on circuits and antennas. There are stories about early inventions, equipment guides, technical articles, and even funny comics. Commenters respect the site’s history and the personal effort behind it. Many enjoy both the educational content and the unique, old-fashioned website style. Some wish for more updates or a modern design, but most agree the site is a treasure chest for anyone who loves radio and electronics.

Finally, we have typ.ing, a website for practicing typing, especially with external keyboards. The site checks if you are on a mobile device and suggests using a physical keyboard for best results. It is simple, clean, and lets you start practicing right away without sign-ups or distractions. The site is linked to ZSA, a maker of ergonomic keyboards. In the comments, people liked the easy, direct design, and the advice to use a real keyboard. Some wanted more advanced features, while others thought the minimal style was just right. There was also talk about the ZSA link being a clever marketing move, but most people appreciated how easy it is to use.
