First, let’s talk about new ways to use Claude 5 generation AI models. A recent article explains "context engineering," which means giving Claude only the information it needs, at the right time, instead of long and strict system prompts. With newer models like Claude Opus 5 and Fable 5, teams found they could remove more than 80% of their old prompts without losing code quality. Before, they wrote many rules to stop Claude from making mistakes, like deleting files or adding too many comments. But now, the models are smart enough to make good decisions with less help.

The article suggests putting tool instructions inside tool descriptions, not in the main prompt, and letting Claude use its own memory system. Too many examples can limit Claude’s creativity, so it's better to keep them simple and clear. Files like specs or mockups can be used as references, since Claude understands structured data well. Main context files should be simple, only adding special warnings when necessary. They also recommend "progressive disclosure," which means Claude loads information only when needed.

In the comments, most users like the idea of trusting newer models and simplifying prompts. Some worry that less guidance could cause mistakes in complex projects, especially where safety is important, like banking or healthcare. Others are excited about tools like "claude doctor" that help check and simplify context files. There’s a debate about how these best practices compare to prompt engineering in other AI models, like GPT-4 or Gemini.

Next, we go to St. Petersburg, Florida, where a 77-year-old man named Carl Gunn is protesting against Flock cameras. These cameras are set up all over the city to record car license plates and other details using AI. The data goes into a big company-run database that police can search, not only in St. Petersburg but across the country. Carl says this is a violation of privacy and does not want to be watched all the time. He protested peacefully by sitting in front of a camera with a sign saying “DOWN WITH FLOCK CAMERAS.” Others supported him, and the police talked to him but did not arrest him.

Many people agree with Carl, saying these cameras could be abused and lead to mass surveillance, like in the UK or China. Some people think cameras help solve crimes, but others feel this technology is hard to stop and call for better laws. Some share tips for avoiding cameras or joining protests. There is surprise that a Republican is leading the protest, showing that privacy worries cross party lines. Many respect Carl’s effort, even if one person alone might not change much.

Now, let’s talk about a creative web project called “Stolen Buttons.” A designer named Anatoly Zenkov visits websites and collects one button from each, showing them all on his page. These buttons include "Log in," "Register," "Buy now," "Accept cookies," and more. All the buttons are real, from real sites, and are shown without changes.

The project shows how different designers make their buttons—some big and colorful, some small and simple, some with icons or animations. By looking at them together, you can see trends in web design and how buttons affect user experience.

Hacker News users enjoy the project. Some remember old button styles and find it inspiring. Others miss the more creative designs from the past. A few discuss whether the designer copies just screenshots or also the code. Some joke about the name "Stolen Buttons," while others say it is more like inspiration or art, not real stealing. Overall, it’s seen as a simple and fun way to look at the details of web design.

Kurt wrote that Fly.io had strong financial months but needed to decide its future direction. The new focus is on "Sprites," which are small, quick cloud computers made for AI agents, not just for human developers. Sprites start fast, have durable storage, and only cost money when running. The team made them easier and safer with new storage and security features.

Kurt believes AI agents will soon do much of the software development work, so Fly.io wants to support them. He will stay as advisor and board member. In the comments, many thank Kurt for his honesty, but some worry that the company might forget regular developers. There is interest in the tech behind Sprites and discussion about whether it’s risky to focus so much on AI agents. Some praise bringing in Scott Johnston, hoping his Docker experience will help Fly.io grow. The community is divided but interested in what will happen next.

Reports are anonymous and checked by a moderator. Companies are listed on a leaderboard for ghosting or for good communication. The site tries to be fair for both sides, and candidates can ask for corrections if needed.

Many Hacker News users think ghosting is a big problem and like the idea of public feedback to push companies to change. Others worry about fake reports hurting reputations. In Minnesota, a new plant is making one ton of ammonia per day for local farms, using wind power. The plant uses wind turbines to power electrolysers, which make hydrogen from water, and then turn it into ammonia for fertilizer. The system adjusts its production based on wind strength, saving money by not building expensive storage. The ammonia can also be turned into urea, another fertilizer, using carbon dioxide from other factories.

This project is part of the University of Minnesota’s research center and works with other partners. It aims to lower fertilizer prices and make supply more stable for local farmers. Many on Hacker News are excited about using wind power for fertilizer and think it will help farmers and the environment. Some worry about reliability when there is not enough wind, and others ask about costs and if the method can replace traditional factories. There is hope that similar plants can be built elsewhere, maybe using solar power too.

Now, some important news about Android development. Google may soon block On-Device ADB (Android Debug Bridge), which could make tools like Shizuku and libadb stop working. This change is not yet official, but a Google developer suggested it on IssueTracker after a security problem was found. The plan is to limit ADB to WiFi connections and block local loopback connections on the device.

The article’s author disagrees with this plan, saying that using ADB badly already requires many manual steps from the user, so blocking On-Device ADB is too much. Many useful apps, especially for developers and people with disabilities, would break if this change happens. In the comments, some agree and think this will hurt Android’s open nature. Others say security is more important and most users never use these features. Some suggest a compromise: block it by default but let advanced users turn it back on. There are worries that this change could make Android more closed, like iOS, and force people to root their devices or use risky workarounds.

Turning to energy storage, GM is investing in Peak Energy, a company making sodium-ion batteries for the U.S. power grid. Sodium-ion batteries hold less energy than lithium-iron phosphate batteries, but they are cheaper, last longer, and work well in high heat. They do not need active cooling, making them easier and cheaper to operate. Sodium is common and easy to find in the U.S., but most battery production is still in China.

Peak Energy uses a material called sodium iron pyrophosphate, which is safe and stable. GM has tested these batteries and found they can last 20 years. The company says that as production grows, prices will drop to match lithium batteries by 2028. Sodium-ion batteries are not meant to replace all others but are a good choice for large, stationary storage.

Many commenters are excited about cheap, abundant materials and hope this will help renewable energy. Some worry about China’s control of the supply chain. Others talk about technical details like high temperature performance and lower maintenance. There is hope that, with GM’s support, this new attempt will succeed and help diversify battery types for the energy grid.

Next, an article shares animations that show how transistors work inside. The author uses a simulator to create videos for different types of transistors, showing electrons and holes as colored dots. The animations help make invisible processes visible—like drift, diffusion, and current flow. Some videos show more realistic, but harder to watch, motion with diffusion included. Others add probes to show current and voltage at each terminal.

Hacker News users say the animations are very helpful for learning electronics. Some suggest adding labels or more transistor types. The author explains his modeling choices in the comments. Teachers and students all agree that tools like this make electronics much easier to understand.

Finally, we have a guide on setting up multicast TV streaming at home. Multicast lets you send live TV or video files to many devices at once without using too much bandwidth. The author explains how to use tools like ffmpeg and yle-dl to stream video files or live TV over the network. For larger networks, the guide covers routed multicast and how to set up multicast routing with IPv6 and FRR.

Many readers are interested in how multicast can save bandwidth and share their own setups. Some warn that multicast may cause problems on WiFi or with some routers if not set up carefully. Others say it’s great for watching TV in several rooms but ask about security and stopping unwanted devices from joining. Some point out that most modern streaming apps use unicast instead, because it’s simpler for the internet. Overall, multicast is seen as powerful but best for special cases like home TV or big office networks.
