## A 3rd World Embedded Engineer Responds to "RISC-V They Should Have Known Better"

- 原文链接: [A 3rd World Embedded Engineer Responds to "RISC-V They Should Have Known Better"](https://rvembedded.com/blog_post/12/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49321717)

This article is a response from an embedded engineer in Trinidad and Tobago to a critical post about RISC-V chips. The author says that while there are fair technical complaints about RISC-V’s design, many critics miss what matters most to people outside the US and Europe.

He explains that in his country, getting hardware is slow and very expensive—sometimes $60 to $200 just for shipping cheap chips. For him and his students, the price of a chip is a big deal; it decides if every student can have their own device or if they just watch one demo. He says RISC-V makes a real difference because it is cheap, available, and free from big company licenses.

The article reviews how RISC-V chips, like the CH32V003 and CH32H417, cover everything from very cheap microcontrollers to more powerful chips that can run Linux and advanced projects. He gives examples of his own projects, like a whistle switch and a web browser on a microcontroller, all done with RISC-V. He says that with under $100, he got all the parts and tools he needed, while just one ARM debugger can cost $200 or more and is much harder to get.

The author disagrees with critics who say one instruction set can’t serve both tiny and powerful chips. He shows that RISC-V lets you use the same base instruction set for everything, so learning one RISC-V chip helps you use others. With ARM, moving to more advanced chips means learning new things and dealing with company rules and contracts.

He also says RISC-V is more open. With ARM, you need special licenses and permission to get advanced features. With RISC-V, anyone can add features, like a memory management unit, without asking or paying extra. This gives much more freedom to engineers everywhere.

About fragmentation (where chips are different in small ways), he admits it is a problem, but he says it is the price for being flexible and letting chips fit many uses. He thinks this is better than being locked out by expensive licenses and rules.

He ends by saying that RISC-V is winning not because it is technically perfect, but because it is cheap, open, and available to people everywhere. This means more people can learn, build, and create products, even in places big companies ignore.

In the comments, some readers agree that price and access are the most important things, especially in poorer countries. They say that RISC-V is helping people learn and build hardware who could not before. Others say technical problems with RISC-V are still real and could slow down its progress, especially for big companies or advanced uses.

A few people worry about fragmentation, saying it could make software harder to write and test. But others reply that having more choices and freedom is worth it, and that good tools can make things easier over time.

Some readers from the US and Europe admit they did not think about how hard it is to get hardware in other places. They say this article opened their eyes to problems they never face. One commenter says it is good to hear from someone outside the usual tech centers.

A few people defend ARM, saying it is still very good for some uses and that its ecosystem is more mature. But most agree that RISC-V’s openness and low cost are big reasons for its growing popularity.

Several readers thank the author for sharing his personal story and say they are inspired to try RISC-V themselves. Others suggest that the tech industry needs to listen more to voices from outside the big markets. Some suggest ways RISC-V could improve, like making extensions simpler or tools even better.

Overall, the discussion shows that while RISC-V is not perfect, its openness and price are changing who can take part in technology—and many think that matters most.

## The Case Against Formal Verification, 50 Years Later

- 原文链接: [The Case Against Formal Verification, 50 Years Later](https://ivan-gavran.github.io/0-social-processes-paper)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49323459)

This article looks at a famous paper from 1979 that argued against using formal verification—the idea of proving that software is correct using math. Today, more people are interested in formal verification again, partly because AI is writing more code for us.

The old paper says that making software like math, with every program proven like a theorem, is not really possible or even how math works. Proofs are just the start; real understanding comes from people talking and testing together. Also, moving real-world needs into formal rules is hard and mistakes can happen. It’s even harder to keep these rules and the code separate, especially as software changes over time. The article adds that, while this is true, modern tools and languages make it easier to check and change rules, and humans are still needed to decide what “correct” means.

The old paper also argued that fully automatic checking is impossible. The article replies that AI tools are getting much better, so maybe this will change soon. Another worry was that “black box” verification (just a YES/NO answer) won’t help programmers learn. The article says this isn’t a strong point because good tools can do more than just say yes or no. 

Some things are still true: real-world systems are messy, and not everything needs strict checking. But as software controls bigger and more important things, having good ways to check it becomes more useful. The article agrees with the paper’s point that software reliability is about more than just proofs—it’s also about good engineering, teamwork, and using lessons from the past.

In the end, the article says formal verification is helpful, even if it’s not perfect, and especially as AI writes more code. It helps people check what the AI did and gives feedback to improve both code and rules.

In the comments, some people agree that full formal verification is too hard or expensive for most projects. They think it’s better for small, critical parts, like the core of a crypto system, than for whole apps. Others point out that the best use of formal methods is to write better specs (rules) and spot mistakes early, not just to “prove” code is correct. Some say that even if full verification is rare, the tools and thinking from formal methods make all software better. There’s debate about whether AI will really make verification easy, or just add new kinds of problems.

A few users share stories about using formal verification at work. Some found it slow and costly, but others say it made their code safer and easier to change later. Many agree that talking with users and other engineers to define what “correct” means is just as important as any proof. Some worry about over-trusting AI or tools, and say humans must still make final decisions. In general, most agree that formal verification is not magic, but it can be a useful part of building better software, especially as systems get more complex.

## Models Are Getting Dumber on Purpose

- 原文链接: [Models Are Getting Dumber on Purpose](https://w4g1.dev/blog/models-are-getting-dumber-on-purpose)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49322695)

The article talks about new AI models that are smaller and use less memory, but they are made this way on purpose. These models are better at math and code problems, but they are not as good at remembering facts. The writer says that earlier, bigger models like GPT-4 had many more parts (“parameters”) and could store more facts. Now, smaller models can solve problems by using clever methods instead of just remembering lots of facts. For example, some models that fit on a normal computer GPU can beat older, much larger models on reasoning tests.

The article explains that facts take up a lot of space in a model, and these facts go out of date quickly. For example, a model trained in 2024 would soon be wrong about new programming libraries or news. But reasoning skills do not get old; math and logic stay the same. So, if a model is trained to be good at procedures and reasoning, it will last longer and not need to be re-trained as often.

Instead of storing every fact, new models use “harnesses.” This means the model gets facts when it needs them, using things like web search, databases, or by reading documents. For example, a coding AI can look up the latest documentation instead of memorizing it. This makes the model smaller and faster, and you can run it on your own computer.

There is another benefit: when a model makes up a fact (a “hallucination”), it is hard to fix. But if the model uses outside sources and cites them, you can check and correct mistakes easily. This is more like a normal bug in computer code, and it can be fixed quickly.

The article thinks that in the future, AI models will be small, smart at reasoning, and always up to date by looking up facts as needed. There will be no need to retrain them just to learn new facts.

In the comment section, some people agree and say this is a smart way to handle fast-changing information. They like the idea that models can be kept up-to-date with fresh data, not just old memories. Others are worried that using outside sources can be slow, or that it is hard to trust the sources the model finds. Some people point out that for some uses, like offline tools or places without internet, it is still useful to have facts inside the model.

A few commenters remember times when AI models would confidently give wrong answers, and they hope this new method will make errors easier to spot and fix. Some think that having small models on personal computers is great for privacy, since your data does not leave your machine. However, other users say that depending on outside data could create new problems if the web changes or sources disappear.

There are also questions about how well the model can “reason” without knowing many facts up front. Some developers mention that this approach is similar to how humans work: we look things up instead of remembering everything. Finally, a few users warn that models will still sometimes make mistakes when mixing facts from different places, so careful checking is still needed.

## SIMD in the 90s: Programming Intel's Pentium MMX

- 原文链接: [SIMD in the 90s: Programming Intel's Pentium MMX](https://pikuma.com/blog/programming-intel-pentium-mmx-simd)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49285096)

This article is about Intel’s Pentium MMX chip from 1997 and how it brought SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) to regular desktop computers. The author explains what MMX was, why it was important, and how it worked for programmers in the 90s.

MMX added 57 new instructions to the Pentium processor. The main idea was to let the CPU do the same operation on several pieces of data at once, instead of processing them one by one. This was especially helpful for things like graphics, audio, and video, where you often have to do the same calculation for many pixels or samples. MMX used special 64-bit registers (called MM0–MM7) which were actually the same as the floating-point registers, but only used the lower 64 bits. This saved space on the chip, but it meant you couldn’t use MMX and floating-point math at the same time.

With MMX, you could write code that added, multiplied, or shifted several numbers in one instruction. For example, instead of adding eight pixels one by one, MMX let you add all eight together at once. It also had “saturating” arithmetic, so if a pixel value got too high, it capped at 255 instead of wrapping around to zero. This was useful for image processing and audio mixing.

MMX was not the first SIMD system. Supercomputers like the ILLIAC IV and Cray-1 in the 60s and 70s used similar ideas, but MMX made SIMD common for home computers. MMX didn’t support floating-point SIMD—that came later with SSE (Streaming SIMD Extensions). To use MMX, software first checked if the CPU supported it using the CPUID instruction.

Games in the late 90s sometimes used MMX for things like texture mapping, alpha blending, and audio, but not as much as you might expect. By the time MMX arrived, 3D graphics cards were starting to take over many of these jobs. Still, some games and programs included special MMX code for better speed if the hardware was available. AMD later made something similar called 3DNow!, but it never became as popular as MMX.

MMX became less important after Intel released SSE and then SSE2, which added wider registers (128 bits) and more powerful SIMD instructions. SSE2 also allowed for both integer and floating-point SIMD, making MMX obsolete. MMX was not a failure, but it was a short step in a longer journey toward modern SIMD in CPUs.

In the comments, many people shared memories of writing assembly code for MMX or learning about SIMD from those days. Some said MMX made them think differently about programming, seeing data as groups instead of single values. Others pointed out MMX’s main problem: it couldn’t do floating-point math and SIMD together, so it was hard to use both in the same program. Some commenters remembered the “EMMS” instruction you had to use to switch back from MMX to floating point, and the bugs that happened if you forgot it.

There were also comments about how MMX was great for some tasks (like image and audio processing), but not as useful for games because of the rise of graphics cards. A few people said that writing MMX code by hand was fun but tricky, and modern compilers can now often do similar optimizations automatically. Some folks mentioned that MMX taught them the basics of vector processing, which is now everywhere in modern CPUs and GPUs. Others discussed how the marketing around “Intel Inside” and “MMX” sometimes made people think their computers would magically get faster, but in reality, only some programs used these features.

Some users discussed differences between MMX and later technologies like SSE, AVX, and ARM’s NEON, noting how newer systems fixed MMX’s main limits. A few people highlighted that MMX was important for its time, even if it didn’t last long, and that it’s a cool piece of computer history. Lastly, there was agreement that MMX was a stepping stone that helped bring advanced computing ideas to everyday programmers.

## St Lucie Nuclear Reactor Unit 1 manually shutdown, 3 control rods drop into core

- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49320856)

St. Lucie Nuclear Power Plant Unit 1 was shut down by workers after three control rods dropped into the reactor core while running at full power. This happened on August 13, 2026, but the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) said it was not an emergency and all systems worked as they should.

The plant’s operators stopped the reactor manually at 9:47 a.m. They did this because three control rods moved unexpectedly into the core. Control rods help control the nuclear reaction. When they drop in, they slow down or stop the reaction to keep things safe. After the shutdown, the plant was put into Mode 3, called Hot Standby, which means the reactor is not making power but can be restarted if needed. The leftover heat, called decay heat, was removed by sending steam to the main condenser using special valves. Unit 2 at the same plant was not affected by this event. The plant’s company, NextEra Energy, said Unit 1 is now back online and running at full power again after fixing the equipment problem.

## MathCode, Mathematical Coding Agent

- 原文链接: [MathCode, Mathematical Coding Agent](https://math-ai-org.github.io/mathcode/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49322330)

MathCode is a new AI tool that helps people turn math problems in simple words into formal Lean 4 code and tries to prove them. The tool works on macOS and Linux and lets users interact with it through the terminal or a browser.

The main idea is that you can ask MathCode to prove math statements, and it will write a theorem in Lean, try to prove it, and save the result. It keeps a fast Lean REPL running, so checking code is quick after the first startup. When a proof is found, it saves the theorem so it can be used again later. You can also save your own rules or assumptions, and MathCode will check them for mistakes.

MathCode connects to online databases to find useful math facts and lemmas, helping it solve harder problems. It can show how theorems connect to each other in a graph using Obsidian, which helps users see how ideas build on each other. The agent interacts with errors—if a proof fails, it tries other ways, fixes mistakes, and keeps going until it succeeds or gives up.

For tricky problems, MathCode splits them into smaller pieces, solves each piece, and puts the answers together. It can run several proof strategies at once and pick the best one. The tool is open-source and based on earlier projects in automated math proving.

In the Hacker News comments, many people were excited to see AI helping with formal math proofs, saying it could make math easier for everyone. Some users worried about the tool only working on macOS and Linux, hoping for a Windows version. Others asked if MathCode could help students learn or if it would mostly help experts.

A few people discussed Lean 4, saying it is powerful but hard to learn, so a good assistant is helpful. Some pointed out that connecting to online lemma databases is a great feature. There were questions about how well the agent understands natural language math questions and if it can always give correct answers.

One user liked the idea of saving theorems for later, saying it could build a big knowledge base. Others wanted to know how MathCode compares to similar tools like Coq or Isabelle. Some were curious about the Obsidian graph, asking if it could help teachers show how math concepts link together. Overall, most comments were positive and interested in seeing how MathCode grows in the future.
