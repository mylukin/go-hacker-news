## What the fuck is w2rt6byl5nf1opmc-a.com?

- 原文链接: [What the fuck is w2rt6byl5nf1opmc-a.com?](https://jack.cab/blog/siterate)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50517602)

A strange web address, w2rt6byl5nf1opmc-a.com, reached number eight on the Majestic Million website list. The author found it while checking which sites linked to his blog.

Majestic ranks websites by how many different networks link to them, rather than how many people visit. This unknown address ranked above Microsoft and GitHub. Yet the author could not even open it because its name did not resolve to a server. Majestic reported more than 241 million links to it from over five million domains. Another link-checking service, Ahrefs, found just 53 links. Almost all the links Majestic counted carried a “nofollow” label, which can limit how search engines use them. Stranger still, its table of link text accounted for only nine links. Six addresses under the same domain also appeared in the rankings, some near the top. The author found records of links from before the domain appeared to be registered. The address also reached number 38 on Tranco, another ranking that uses Majestic data. One possible explanation was a fake entry meant to reveal who copied the list. Other ideas included someone cheating the ranking system or websites showing special content only to tracking bots. The author found no clear answer.

No Hacker News comments were provided, so the following are possible discussion points, not reported reader views. A developer might first suspect a bug in how Majestic collects or counts links. Someone working with search rankings might instead ask whether the links are hidden from other checking tools. A more careful reader might warn that the gap between two services does not prove either explanation. Another question is whether the list measures popularity at all, since links are not visits. People using these lists for research might worry about false entries affecting their results. Tranco’s position raises a related concern: a second list can repeat problems from its source. On the other hand, one strange result does not show that every entry is wrong. Comparing several independent sources could help reveal where the numbers stop making sense. The key lesson is simple: a precise rank is not the same as reliable evidence.

## Amazon's Discount Illusion: 1 in 3 "Deals" Offered No Savings– 1 in 10 Cost More

- 原文链接: [Amazon's Discount Illusion: 1 in 3 "Deals" Offered No Savings– 1 in 10 Cost More](http://groundworkcollaborative.org/news/amazons-decoy-deals-groundwork-investigation-reveals-nearly-1-in-3-black-friday-and-cyber-monday-deals-advertised-by-amazon-offered-no-savings-to-shoppers-nearly-1-in-10-w/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50517488)

A Groundwork Collaborative study says many Amazon holiday “deals” offered no savings compared with recent prices. It checked more than 350 products Amazon promoted for Black Friday and Cyber Monday in 2024 and 2025.

The researchers compared sale prices with prices from the month before each event. Nearly one in three products had already been available for the same price or less. Nearly one in ten actually cost more during the sale. That means waiting for a big shopping event could leave some buyers paying extra.

For example, Hunter rain boots were advertised at $126.99 during Black Friday 2025. Nine days before the sale, they had cost $79.97. Waiting meant paying about 59% more. A pair of UGG boots appeared in the Cyber Monday sale at $179.98. About four weeks earlier, they had cost $98.40, making the sale price about 83% higher. Among products that cost more during the sales, the middle price increase was 19%.

Groundwork says Amazon removed the deal pages used in the study after learning about the research. The group wants stronger action against sale claims that mislead shoppers. It also wants shops to show each product’s lowest price from the previous 30 days when advertising a discount.

No Hacker News comments were supplied, so the following points are possible discussion views, not reported reader opinions. From a shopper’s view, a “best deal” label may suggest that waiting will bring a lower price. A seller might argue that changing prices alone does not prove a sale claim is false. Someone checking the research might ask how the products were chosen and whether they represent Amazon’s wider sales. Another question is whether a brief price drop weeks earlier is a fair measure of a later deal. Still, comparing only with a high list price may give buyers the wrong idea about their savings.

For software developers, this is a useful case for tools that track prices over time. Supporters of stronger rules might see a 30-day price history as a simple way to help shoppers. Others might ask how such rules would handle different sellers, sizes, or product versions. The practical lesson is to check a product’s price history rather than trust the sale label alone.

## The Secret ThinkPad Powerbutton Code to Bring Dead Laptops Back to Life (2007)

- 原文链接: [The Secret ThinkPad Powerbutton Code to Bring Dead Laptops Back to Life (2007)](https://masnick.com/posts/the-secret-thinkpad-powerbutton-code-to-bring-dead-laptops-back-to-life/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50516473)

In this 2007 post, Mike Masnick shares a strange power button trick that brought his dead ThinkPad back to life. His nearly new laptop stopped working about an hour before he was due to give a talk.

The laptop showed no lights and did not respond to the power button. It was also very hot, even though he had left it asleep. Removing the battery and putting it back did not help. He called IBM support, where a worker suggested a surprising set of steps. First, unplug the charger and remove the battery. Then press the power button ten times, with one second between each press.

Next, hold the power button down for thirty seconds. Put the battery back in and press the power button again. After these steps, Masnick’s laptop started working again. The support worker called this “static discharge,” meaning the release of built-up electric charge. But he could not explain exactly why it worked. The post gives one success story, not proof that the same steps will fix every dead laptop. Masnick jokes that this is better than a secret code in a video game.

In the blog comments provided here, many readers say the trick worked for them too. One reader even says they work for Lenovo and still found the advice helpful. Others describe saving money because they no longer needed a repair or a new laptop. Some report success with other laptop brands, suggesting the idea is not only useful for ThinkPads. However, several readers question the support worker’s explanation.

They suggest the trick drains power left inside the machine, rather than removing static charge. Some say a single press or a long hold is enough, while others say only the full set of steps worked. Several readers report that the trick did nothing, so the happy stories are not the whole picture. Some found a different answer, such as removing and putting back the memory or taking out the CD drive. Many also feared losing work they had not copied elsewhere, adding a clear reminder to keep a second copy of important files.

## Universal XSS in uBlock Origin from website-driven element picker

- 原文链接: [Universal XSS in uBlock Origin from website-driven element picker](https://iter.ca/post/ubo-uxss/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513160)

A security researcher found a now-fixed flaw in uBlock Origin for Firefox that could let a harmful website run code on other websites. The attack needed one user click and used the tool for choosing parts of a page to block.

A similar but less serious issue also affected uBlock Origin Lite. The tool, called the element picker, normally helps users create their own blocking rules. It runs inside a small page loaded from the extension, whose address includes a random ID. A website could learn that ID by watching a request the extension made while replacing an ad script. With that address, the website could load its own copy of the picker inside the page. The picker accepted messages from the website without checking that they came from a safe source. Those messages could set a blocking rule and turn on the Create button. By adding a line break in one message field, the attacker could slip in an extra, trusted rule. That rule could make the extension run JavaScript code on a different website. The attacker could hide the picker and place its Create button where the user was likely to click. The author did not build that hidden-click part, but explained how it could complete the attack. One fix added a secret key so the picker could check that messages came from the right source. The author says an AI model found the main flaw, which they then checked, while another AI wrote most of the test code.

No Hacker News comments were provided, so the following points are discussion topics, not reported reader views. For users, the first concern is making sure the extension is up to date. For extension developers, the case shows why messages from websites need careful checks. Another concern is how much power a tool gains when it can change content across many sites. At the same time, ad blockers can help protect users from harmful ads and unwanted tracking. That makes this a reason to improve their safety, not proof that users should stop using them. The need for a click matters because simply visiting the page would not complete this attack. Still, a hidden button could turn an ordinary click into something the user never meant to approve. The AI angle raises another useful question: how much can these tools help people find security flaws? Here, the human check remained important, because a suggested attack still needed testing before it could be treated as real.

## Researchers track whole body movement with just a smartwatch

- 原文链接: [Researchers track whole body movement with just a smartwatch](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/11/researchers-track-whole-body-movement-just-smartwatch)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50486745)

Cornell researchers have built a system that tracks whole-body movement using just a smartwatch. Called EchoMotion, it uses sound and a small AI model instead of cameras or extra body sensors.

Normal watch sensors mainly measure how the watch itself moves. EchoMotion uses the watch’s speaker to send out sounds that people cannot hear. Its microphone then picks up echoes from the person’s moving body. The AI uses these echoes to work out the positions of 12 body joints. Without cameras, the system avoids taking pictures of the wearer and nearby people. A 30-minute test on a Samsung Galaxy Watch 6 used about 5% to 6% of its battery. The team first collected data from 10 people doing 18 exercises with a specially built watch. They then tested an ordinary Galaxy Watch 6 with 15 new people. For new users, the average error in joint position was less than four inches. A one-time, 12-minute setup for each person reduced that error to about 2.4 inches. The same AI model also worked on two other watch models without further training. Tests in different places found little trouble from furniture or road noise, but nearby people caused some problems in a narrow hallway. The team hopes to use the system to count exercises and help people improve how they move.

No Hacker News comments were included, so I cannot report what readers there said. The following points are possible reactions, not comments from the site. For developers, this is an interesting example of a small AI model doing useful work with ordinary hardware. For people who dislike cameras, sound-based tracking could offer a welcome choice. Still, a system that records someone’s movements all day raises its own privacy questions. The small study and limited set of exercises also leave questions about how well it handles everyday life. An error of a few inches might be fine for counting exercises but too large for checking some small movements. Use during recovery from an injury would need further testing, rather than being treated as ready today. Busy places remain another challenge, and the short battery test does not show what all-day use would cost. Overall, the work looks promising for exercise tracking, while reliable tracking throughout a whole day remains a future goal.

## Soldier files whistleblower complaint over Iran strike that killed 6 U.S. troops

- 原文链接: [Soldier files whistleblower complaint over Iran strike that killed 6 U.S. troops](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/15/politics/army-soldier-whistleblower-complaint)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50517838)

An Army soldier who survived an Iranian drone attack in Kuwait has filed a complaint against the Pentagon, according to the supplied CNN report. He says military leaders ignored warnings about poor protection before the attack killed six American soldiers.

Major Stephen Ramsbottom sent his complaint to committees in both houses of Congress. The March 1 attack hit a temporary military operations center at Shuaiba port. Dozens of people were hurt, including Ramsbottom. He says he raised concerns about attacks from the air in several meetings. According to him, leaders answered that protection was not something they needed to worry about.

Leaders later said they would try to get equipment to stop drones. Ramsbottom says it never arrived, and he does not know whether anyone ordered it. A source told CNN that concrete barriers could protect the site from car bombs but not attacks from above. CNN also reported that troops received no warning before the strike.

The complaint says the site had no doctor or permanent medical station. There was only one ambulance. Ramsbottom argues that the site was prepared for older threats, not modern drone attacks. CNN said it had asked the Pentagon and the congressional committees for comment.

No Hacker News comments were supplied, so we cannot report readers’ views or say which opinions received the most votes. The article itself does offer several different views. Ramsbottom calls the attack a planning failure and includes himself among those responsible. His point is that the military needs to learn from what went wrong. He also questions whether earlier ideas about protection still work against drones.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth offered a different account after the attack, saying American air defenses were strong. He said one weapon got through and hit a building that had protection. That leaves a key disagreement over whether the protection matched the danger. Senator Richard Blumenthal has asked for safety records and a private briefing to examine those decisions. The central question is whether leaders took reasonable steps to protect troops after concerns were raised.

## OOS – an open operating system for the Rigol Scopes

- 原文链接: [OOS – an open operating system for the Rigol Scopes](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/oos-an-open-operating-system-for-the-rigol-scopes/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50490159)

OOS is a free, open source operating system for Rigol DHO800 and DHO900 scopes. These tools show electrical signals on a screen, helping people test circuits and find problems.

The project uses Linux 6.12 and a new scope app written in Rust. It runs from the scope’s SD card, alongside the original system. Users can switch back to the original software. OOS leaves the factory measurement settings unchanged and saves its own settings separately. Still, this is early software, and users try it at their own risk.

At first, it had only run on the developer’s DHO924S. Some features have only been tested with computer-made signals, and its measurements are not checked against a standard. The app offers automatic measurements, signal recording, and several ways to catch signal changes. It can also read messages sent through connections such as USB, SPI, and CAN. Users can apply maths to signals, study noise, and compare saved signals.

Remote control works through a browser or software commands over a network. However, its telnet connection gives full system access without a password, so it needs a trusted network. Installation options include a USB drive or a second SD card, with a full backup strongly advised.

In the EEVblog comments, some people wanted more test reports before trying it themselves. One user reported success on a DHO804 and thanked the developer for quickly adding keyboard arrow keys. That small change made fixing mistakes in long Wi-Fi passwords much easier. Other users felt the screen showed too much detail and the text was too small. The developer added larger text options but agreed that reducing screen clutter was a harder problem.

One tester found that remote viewing slowed down the scope’s own display. The developer traced this to network code that paused screen updates and reported much better speed after a fix. Another user suggested getting the correct date and time from the network. A reply supported the idea but wanted it off by default, and the developer added that choice. There was real excitement too: one commenter said the project could make them buy a Rigol scope so they could add their own measurements.

## “…and the proof is checked in Lean”

- 原文链接: [“…and the proof is checked in Lean”](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/12/and-the-proof-is-checked-in-lean/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50515058)

The post asks what “the proof is checked in Lean” really tells us about a math result. It looks at AI-written papers from OpenAI and explains why having Lean code is not enough.

Lean is a tool that checks math proofs, but first someone must check that it is proving the right claim. A small mistake in the claim can change the whole problem. For example, allowing zero in the wrong place changes a statement about Fermat’s Last Theorem. The author says some OpenAI papers include Lean proofs of smaller results, not the paper’s main result. Checking the meaning can also mean reading thousands of lines of new math definitions.

Next, the code must actually build and pass Lean’s checks. The author warns that unknown code should run in a safe, separate space because it could harm your files. Very large projects may also need far more memory than a normal laptop has. Then, a tool called `lake comparator` checks whether the proof matches a trusted copy of the claim. It also checks that the proof does not quietly add new rules that make the answer true.

Lean itself can have bugs, and the post describes bugs that have been fixed. The author says `lake comparator --paranoid` uses several separate proof checkers, making one hidden bug much less likely to fool the whole test. Even after all these checks pass, the written paper may still contain mistakes or use a different argument.

No Hacker News comments were supplied, so the following are possible discussion points, not reported reader views. A hopeful reader might see this as a strong path toward checking AI-made math. A careful reader might answer that checking the wrong claim gives little comfort. A developer might compare this to software tests that pass while testing the wrong thing. Someone building AI tools might want these checks to run automatically before any result is shared.

A security-minded reader might welcome the warning about running unknown code safely. A researcher with limited money might worry about the cost of checking huge projects. Another reader might value separate proof checkers because they reduce trust in any single program. Someone trying to understand the math might still want a clear written argument, not just a passing check. The useful question for discussion is therefore not simply whether Lean code exists, but exactly what it proves and which checks it passed.

## Qwen4-Max

- 原文链接: [Qwen4-Max](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen4-max)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513215)

Alibaba has introduced Qwen4-Max, an AI model built to handle long work projects rather than just answer questions. The company presents it as a teammate for coding, design, research, and office work.

Qwen says the model has 2.4 trillion parameters, the learned values inside it, but uses only part of them for each step. It can understand images and work with up to one million tokens, the small pieces into which text is split. Its new design aims to find useful information in long inputs while keeping computing costs down. In Qwen’s Work360 test, the average cost per task fell from $4.43 to $1.67 compared with the previous model. These are company-reported results, so they do not show what every user should expect.

The examples include building 3D scenes in Blender and making playable games with Unity, Unreal Engine, and Godot. In one manufacturing example, Qwen says the human time needed to program work on a part fell from 475 minutes to 24 minutes. For finance work, it can produce spreadsheets and slides with links back to the data sources. Legal and medical examples show it sorting large sets of records and linking its findings to supporting information.

For longer projects, a feature called Live Delivery gives users an early working version and lets them guide later changes. Qwen also describes a 48-hour run that used an automated lab to make and test a new liquid for cooling computer chips. A separate drug research project produced a possible new molecule, but it still needs lab testing and is not a proven treatment. Max is available through Qwen’s services, while smaller models are planned for open-source release.

Developers on small teams may welcome lower costs, especially if the model needs fewer steps to finish a task. More careful buyers may want outside tests before trusting the company’s results. For long-running work, an important question is how much time people must spend checking and correcting the output. People who want to run models on their own machines may be more interested in the promised smaller releases than Max itself. The main question is whether these tools save time in daily work, beyond the company’s chosen examples.
