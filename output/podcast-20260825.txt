First, the FDA has approved a new wearable device called the Libre Duo 10 Day. This is the first device in the US that lets people with diabetes see both their blood sugar and ketone levels together, all the time. The device is for people aged 2 and up, and it checks these levels every minute, day and night, using a small sensor under the skin. The data goes wirelessly to your smartphone, showing trends and sending alerts if ketones get too high. This is important because high ketones can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis, which is very dangerous. Before this, people had to check ketones with a separate test, and these only gave one result at a time. The FDA approved the device after six studies with over 600 people. In the comments, people called this a big step for diabetes care. Many shared personal experiences, saying real-time ketone alerts could save lives, especially for children. Some asked about price, insurance coverage, and how accurate the readings are. Others discussed privacy and data security, since the device connects to phones. Most agreed this device could make life much safer and easier for people with diabetes.

Next, Apple just announced two new chips: the M6 and the M5 Ultra. The M6 is used in the new Mac mini, while the M5 Ultra is in the new Mac Studio. The M6 is Apple’s first 2-nanometer chip. It has a 12-core CPU and 12-core GPU, plus a Dual 16-core Neural Engine for faster AI. It supports up to 32GB memory and faster memory bandwidth. The M5 Ultra is for professionals, built with a quad-die design that joins four chips together, giving it up to 36 CPU cores, 80 GPU cores, and 512GB of memory. It also has a 32-core Neural Engine for heavy AI and video work. Both chips are made for running and training AI models locally, and Apple says they are energy efficient. In the comments, people are impressed by the tech progress, especially the new chip designs. Some wonder if everyday users will notice the speed, and if software will use all this new power. High memory options are good for science and big AI, but most people won’t need so much. There are worries about price, upgrade options, and how well Apple’s AI tools will work with open-source software. Overall, people see these chips as a big technical leap, but want to see real-world tests.

OpenAI has released a new AI chip called Jalapeño, designed for large language models. The chip was built with Broadcom in only 16 months, which is much faster than usual. Jalapeño uses HBM4 memory and can run many open-source AI models. In tests, it beats Nvidia and AMD chips in many tasks, with better performance per watt. OpenAI focused on power efficiency because data centers care more about power than space or cost now. The chip uses a flexible design to keep all parts busy, and it connects up to 2,048 chips together. OpenAI also made a new programming language, Gluon, for writing code for Jalapeño, and used AI tools like Codex to help. In the comments, people are impressed by the speed of Jalapeño’s development and the close work between hardware and software teams. Another story explains new research about black holes. Many people think the singularity inside a black hole is a point, but new work shows it is actually a surface. In general relativity, if two people fall into a black hole from different sides, they can’t meet at the singularity, even if they are close. The singularity is a surface where physics breaks down, not just a single point. For spinning black holes, things are more complex, but the result is similar. The research also talks about how information might be stored on that surface, linking to the holographic principle. In the comments, many are surprised by this new idea, saying it changes their picture of black holes. Some bring up textbooks and older papers, noting this is not well known. There is talk about how strange space is inside black holes, and questions about testing these ideas. Most agree black holes are still very mysterious, and we have a lot to learn.

On the environmental front, bomb fishing is still causing big damage to Indonesia’s coral reefs, especially in the Spermonde Archipelago. Fishers use homemade bombs to catch fish, but these bombs destroy coral, turning reefs into fields of rubble. Researchers found more than 8,000 bombs go off each year in this area. Since 1990, about 75% of coral has disappeared. Scientists used audio recorders and open-source software to track the blasts. Bomb fishing is often done by middle-income fishers, not just the very poor, because the equipment costs money. Weak laws and little enforcement make it hard to stop. In the comments, people are shocked this still happens, and point to poverty and weak law enforcement as root causes. Some are hopeful that new audio technology can help, but note the problem is global. Others say real solutions must come from local communities.

Finally, there is a security issue in Python when using `str.lower()` with domain names. The problem comes from how Python changes letters to lowercase, which is important for following domain name rules. Internet standards use Unicode, but domain names must use only ASCII letters. The older IDNA 2003 standard uses Unicode 3.2.0 for “case folding.” Python’s `str.lower()` uses the Unicode version from your Python build, which may be newer, leading to different results for some characters. The Python team fixed this by making sure domain names are lowercased using the old Unicode rules, not the current ones. In the comments, many people are surprised that such a small thing could cause big security problems. Some share examples from their own work, and others discuss how hard it is for programmers to know about these issues. There is also talk about other languages, and the need to test with strange characters, not just English. Most agree Unicode is powerful but tricky for security.
