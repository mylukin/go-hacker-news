## The new rules of context engineering for Claude 5 generation models

- 原文链接: [The new rules of context engineering for Claude 5 generation models](https://claude.com/blog/the-new-rules-of-context-engineering-for-claude-5-generation-models)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49051361)

This article talks about new ways to give Claude 5 generation models the information they need to work well, a process called "context engineering." The main idea is that as Claude models get better, you don’t need to give them as many detailed rules or instructions as before.

The team found they could remove more than 80% of the system prompt for Claude Code on the newest models, like Claude Opus 5 and Fable 5, without losing quality in code tasks. Before, they had to add many strict rules to stop Claude from making mistakes, like deleting files or adding too many comments. Now, these models are smart enough to use judgment and figure out what the user wants, even with less guidance. Instead of packing all the information up front, they recommend giving Claude only what it needs at the right moment. For example, code review steps are now separate skills Claude can call when needed, not always loaded.

They also suggest that you don’t have to repeat instructions everywhere. Put tool instructions in the tool descriptions, not in the main prompt. Let Claude’s memory system handle saving important things instead of making many notes in special files. When you give examples, be careful—too many examples can actually limit how creative Claude is. It’s better to design your tools and instructions clearly, so Claude knows how to use them. Use files as references, like specs or mockups, because Claude understands code and structured data best.

The article recommends keeping your main context files, like CLAUDE.md, simple and focused. Only add special warnings or tips that Claude wouldn’t know just by looking at the code. Use “progressive disclosure,” which means Claude only loads information when needed, not all at once. For skills, break them into small files and only add strict rules if it’s very important. Use code files and real project data as references, instead of long written descriptions.

In the comments, many users like the idea of simplifying prompts and trust that newer models can handle more on their own. Some say this makes it easier to keep projects organized and reduces confusion. Others ask if less guidance could sometimes lead to mistakes, especially in big or complex projects. A few users share that they still like to add clear rules when safety is very important, such as in banking or healthcare code. Some developers point out that giving too much freedom can make model outputs less predictable, while others believe models will get even better at understanding context over time. There’s also discussion about how to teach teams these new best practices and how to test if fewer rules really work in different industries. Some are excited about the new “claude doctor” tool that helps check and simplify context files automatically. Finally, a few are curious how these changes compare to prompt engineering in other AI models, like GPT-4 or Gemini.

Police can search this data, not just in St. Petersburg, but across the country, to find cars, suspects, or even details like bumper stickers. Some people fear that police could use these cameras to watch protestors or even stalk people. There have been cases where this technology led to wrongful arrests.

Carl Gunn says he is a law-abiding citizen and does not want to be watched all the time. He feels this is against his right to privacy. He protested by sitting in front of a camera with a sign that says “DOWN WITH FLOCK CAMERAS.” Other people showed support, bringing him drinks and cheering him on. The police talked to him but did not arrest him.

Gunn was inspired by people who damage cameras, but he chose a peaceful protest. He says the company is making money off people’s data, and he never agreed to this. There are about 50 cameras in St. Petersburg, and some apps and websites now help people avoid them. Civil rights groups like the ACLU say these cameras are a big step towards mass surveillance and could change personal freedom in the U.S.

Commenters on Hacker News have mixed feelings. Some agree with Carl Gunn, saying these cameras are a big risk for privacy and could be abused. They worry about the government or companies watching everyone without clear rules. Others point out that cameras can help solve crimes, making neighborhoods safer. A few people say this kind of technology is hard to stop, and that people should demand better laws and rules instead of protesting. Some mention that similar surveillance is already common in other countries, like the UK and China, and wonder if the U.S. is heading in that direction. Others share tips for protecting privacy, such as using apps to avoid cameras or joining local protests. - HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49051369)

Fly.io’s CEO, Kurt Mackey, is stepping down and Scott Johnston (former Docker CEO) is taking over. The article explains why this change is happening and what it means for Fly.io’s future.

Kurt says Fly.io had strong financial months, but there was concern in the tech community about the company’s future. He admits Fly.io was having an “identity crisis” and needed to decide what kind of company it wants to be. The main change is a new focus on “Sprites,” which are small, cloud computers made for AI agents (not just human developers). Sprites can be spun up quickly, have durable storage, and only cost money when they’re running. This is different from regular cloud servers, which are harder to use and cost more even when idle.

Kurt believes that AI agents will soon do much of the software development work, so cloud platforms need to be built for them—not just humans. He explains that Sprites fit this new world better than traditional cloud services. The team has improved Sprites with better storage (Sprite Block Device) and new security features (Connectors) to make them easier and safer for agents to use. Fly.io raised more money to focus on this direction, and now Scott Johnston will lead the next stage. Kurt will stay as an advisor and board member, helping with product design.

In the Hacker News comments, some people thank Kurt for his transparency and honesty. Many say the post is well-written and interesting. Some are worried about the new focus on AI agents and think Fly.io might lose its appeal for regular developers. Others are curious about the tech behind Sprites and ask for more details. A few users wonder if betting so much on AI agents is risky, since the market is still new and unproven. Some praise the decision to bring in Scott Johnston, saying his experience at Docker could help Fly.io grow. Others hope Fly.io will still support traditional apps and not forget about their current users. There’s respect for Kurt’s leadership and the open way he talked about mistakes and changes. Some compare this shift to other times in tech when big changes happened, like the move from desktop to cloud. Overall, the community is interested but divided about the future direction.

The site lets candidates report companies that did not send any update or rejection after an interview, and also lets people praise companies that communicated well.

The website keeps everything anonymous—people do not need to give their name or email. Each report is checked by a moderator before it is shown to the public. There is also a list for companies that gave clear answers, even if it was a rejection, to show good behavior. The site explains that just not replying to a job application does not count as ghosting; it has to be after an interview. Candidates should wait for the time the company promised and send a follow-up before reporting. If a company thinks a report is wrong, or if a candidate wants to correct something, they can ask for a review. The goal is to make the process fair for both sides and to help people know which companies respect candidates’ time.

## Producing ammonia and fertiliser using wind power in Morris, Minnesota

- 原文链接: [Producing ammonia and fertiliser using wind power in Morris, Minnesota](https://ammoniaenergy.org/articles/flexible-renewable-ammonia-demonstrator-now-operational-in-minnesota/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49050735)

A new plant in Morris, Minnesota is making ammonia and fertilizer using wind power. This is the first season it is running, aiming to produce one ton of low-carbon ammonia per day for local farms.

The plant uses wind turbines to power machines called electrolysers. These machines make hydrogen from water, which is then used in a chemical process to make ammonia. The system can change how much ammonia it makes depending on how much wind energy is available. New control systems help the plant work smoothly, even when the wind changes.

The plant is part of the University of Minnesota’s research center and works with other groups like RTI International and Casale. The main goals are to lower fertilizer prices, make supply more stable, and keep money in the state. This project is bigger than a small test plant built in 2013, and there are plans to let farmer co-ops own similar plants in the future. The ammonia made here can be stored or mixed with carbon dioxide from other factories to make urea, another type of fertilizer. Right now, there are no other big fertilizer factories in Minnesota, so this project is important for local farmers. If farmers know they can get a steady, fair price for fertilizer, they can plan better for each year’s crops.

Experts working on the project say it is special because most chemical plants run at a steady speed, but green energy like wind is not always steady. Instead of building expensive storage for the hydrogen, the plant changes how fast it works, which saves money and energy. They hope more plants like this will be built, with help from the government and loans for local groups.

In the Hacker News comments, many people are excited about using wind power for fertilizer and think this is a good step for cleaner farming. Some say that making fertilizer locally can help farmers and reduce costs. Others worry about how well the plant will work when there is not enough wind, and if it will be reliable in the long term. A few people ask about the costs compared to big commercial factories and if this idea can work in other places. Some commenters want to know if this method can replace traditional fertilizer production everywhere, or if it will only work in places with lots of wind. There are also questions about how much storage is needed and if the technology is safe and easy for farmers to use. A few people share ideas about using other renewable energy sources, like solar, for similar projects. Overall, the discussion is hopeful but also points out challenges that still need answers.

## Android May Soon Restrict On-Device ADB

- 原文链接: [Android May Soon Restrict On-Device ADB](https://kitsumed.github.io/blog/posts/android-may-soon-restrict-on-device-adb/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49045159)

Google may soon change how Android allows developers to use ADB (Android Debug Bridge) directly on their devices, which could make tools like Shizuku and libadb stop working. This news comes from a Google IssueTracker thread, not an official announcement, but a core ADB developer suggested limiting ADB connections to only WiFi, blocking local connections on the same device.

The article explains that ADB is a tool for developers to control Android devices, usually by connecting a phone to a computer. Over time, people have found ways to use ADB directly on their phones, called "On-Device ADB," using apps like Termux or projects like Shizuku. This is useful for developers, power users, and people with special needs. For example, the author made a call recording app that helps with disabilities, and some users use it for personal reasons, like saving voicemails from loved ones.

Recently, a big security problem (CVE-2026-0073) showed that ADB connections could be hacked. To fix this, Google is thinking about letting developers choose which network ADB can use. However, a Google engineer also suggested blocking all local (loopback) ADB connections, saying they could be used by bad apps to gain extra powers.

The blog's author disagrees, explaining that for an app to use ADB in a bad way, a user would still have to do many manual steps—like turning on USB debugging, enabling wireless ADB, and entering codes. Bad apps cannot do this alone. Most risks only exist if the user is already doing advanced things and knows what they are doing. The author believes a full block of On-Device ADB is too much. Instead, there should be a setting that advanced users can turn on, which stays on after a reboot.

The author lists many popular apps and tools that would break if this change happens. They say removing this feature would harm many power users and developers, not just stop bad actors.

In the Hacker News comments, some users agree with the article, saying this would hurt Android’s open nature and make life harder for developers and power users. They point out that many useful apps, especially for accessibility, would stop working. Others say Google is just trying to improve security, and that most users never use On-Device ADB, so Google’s choice makes sense. Some people worry this could be another step toward a closed system, making Android more like iOS. A few users think there should be a compromise: block On-Device ADB by default, but let advanced users turn it back on if they really need it. Some also mention that blocking loopback ADB will push more people to root their devices or use risky workarounds. There are concerns that if Google makes changes without listening to feedback, they might break tools that many small groups depend on. Overall, the discussion shows a split between wanting more security and keeping Android open and flexible for developers and power users.

## GM Backs Sodium Ion Batteries for U.S. Grid Storage

- 原文链接: [GM Backs Sodium Ion Batteries for U.S. Grid Storage](https://spectrum.ieee.org/sodium-ion-battery-peak-energy)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49051947)

GM is investing in a company called Peak Energy, which wants to make sodium-ion batteries for storing electricity on the U.S. power grid. The article explains how sodium-ion batteries could be a strong option for grid storage, even though past U.S. efforts have failed and Chinese companies now lead the field.

Peak Energy plans to build a big factory in California, making batteries that can store lots of energy for long periods. While sodium-ion batteries hold less energy than the popular lithium-iron phosphate (LFP) batteries, Peak Energy says their batteries are cheaper to use over the years because they last longer and can handle higher temperatures. Their design does not need active cooling, making them easier and cheaper to operate, especially in hot places or remote areas.

Sodium is common and easy to find, unlike lithium, which is often mined in far-away places with environmental and human rights concerns. The U.S. has a good supply of raw sodium, but China still controls much of the processing and manufacturing. Peak Energy uses sodium iron pyrophosphate (NFPP) for their batteries, a material that is safe, stable, and already popular with big Chinese battery makers. This means these new batteries can be built in many existing factories.

GM has tested these batteries and found they work well, even in high heat, and can last 20 years without expensive cooling systems. The batteries are also very efficient, losing less energy when charging and discharging. Peak Energy expects that, as production increases, the price of sodium-ion batteries will become as low as LFP batteries by around 2028.

Peak Energy already has projects planned with power companies and will supply batteries for the grid in several U.S. states. The company says sodium-ion batteries are not meant to replace all other batteries but are a good choice where cost, safety, and long life matter more than size or weight.

In the comments, many people are excited about sodium-ion batteries because they use cheap, abundant materials and could make energy storage more affordable. Some worry about the fact that much of the battery supply chain is still in China, even if the U.S. has the raw materials. Others point out that while sodium-ion batteries are not as powerful as lithium batteries, for grid storage, size and weight do not matter as much, so lower cost and longer life are bigger advantages.

A few commenters discuss technical details, like how sodium-ion batteries work better at high temperatures and do not need complex cooling, which lowers maintenance and energy use. Some bring up the history of failed battery startups and ask if Peak Energy can really compete with big Chinese companies. Others are hopeful that, with GM’s support, this attempt might succeed where others have failed.

There is also talk about how diversifying battery types is important for the energy grid, so the world is not dependent on just one material or country. Some point out that, if sodium-ion technology grows, it could help lower the price for all battery storage and speed up the switch to renewable energy. Most agree that even if sodium-ion batteries do not take over the market, they could fill an important gap and help the U.S. catch up in grid storage.

The author uses a semiconductor simulator to make these animations and posts videos for different types of transistors: NPN BJTs, n-channel MOSFETs, and n-channel JFETs. In each animation, blue dots are electrons and red dots are holes, showing how charge carriers move. When a blue and red dot meet, a white flash appears—this is called a recombination event. The first set of videos shows carrier movement with both drift and diffusion combined, but does not show diffusion separately. The next set adds diffusion, making the animations more accurate, but also harder to watch because the motion is more random. The last set of animations includes probes that show the current and voltage at each terminal of the transistor, giving a clearer picture of what’s happening during operation. The goal is to help people see what is usually invisible in semiconductor devices.

## Multicast TV Distribution on My Home Network

- 原文链接: [Multicast TV Distribution on My Home Network](https://www.apalrd.net/posts/2026/isp_mcast/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49051463)

This article talks about how to set up multicast TV streaming on a home network. The author explains how multicast can help send live TV or video files to many devices at the same time without using too much bandwidth.

The article starts by saying multicast is different from normal network traffic. In multicast, data is sent to many devices at once, so you don’t need to send the same video to each device separately. This is great for streaming live TV or videos in a home. The author uses open-source tools like ffmpeg to send video files over the network. If your devices can play the video format, you can stream it directly and save computer power. If not, you can use ffmpeg to change the video to a more common format before streaming.

The author shows how to stream a video file (like “big buck bunny”) using ffmpeg. They give commands for streaming directly and for streaming with transcoding (changing the video format). The article also explains how to stream live TV from a Finnish broadcaster, YLE, using a tool called yle-dl and ffmpeg together.

For bigger networks with many subnets, the writer suggests using “routed multicast.” This means multicast traffic can move across different parts of your network. The author shares example settings for FRR, which helps manage multicast routing with IPv6. There are commands to set up source-specific multicast, which makes things easier and more secure.

Finally, the article includes an example of sending a video file over routed multicast, with options to control how far the packets travel (using a hop limit).

In the comment section, many readers are interested in how multicast works and how it can save bandwidth. Some people share their own setups for streaming TV at home. A few users warn that multicast can cause problems on WiFi networks and switches if not set up carefully, especially without IGMP snooping. Others mention that not all home routers handle multicast well, and it can slow down the network if you are not careful. Some commenters like the idea for watching TV in several rooms, while others ask about security and ways to stop unwanted devices from joining the stream. One person says they tried something similar at work, but found it harder than expected because of network hardware limits. Another user points out that modern streaming apps often use unicast (one-to-one connections) instead, because it is simpler for the internet. Overall, readers think multicast is powerful, but it works best for special cases, like home TV or big company networks.
