Hello everyone, this is the 2026-08-20 episode of Hacker News Daily Podcast. Today, we bring you a mix of important news and deep discussions from the tech world.

First, we look at the big GitHub outage on August 17. The downtime lasted almost 8 hours and affected developers and companies around the world. This was GitHub’s second major problem in August. The cause was a part of their main data center not scaling up as needed when traffic hit a new high. This failure spread to other parts of the system, breaking authentication, APIs, and Copilot. The recovery took time, with Copilot being the slowest because a bug made too many retries, making things worse.

GitHub said this was not because of bad code or a mistake in configuration, but because their systems could not keep up with very fast growth—the number of monthly commits doubled in just a few months. Since then, they have added a lot of new hardware, moved more services to Azure, and are working on ways to let more people read data at the same time. They are also splitting up important services to avoid one failure breaking everything. GitHub is putting rules in place to stop retry storms and improving alerts during busy times. They say they are serious about fixing these problems and want to win back user trust.

In the comments, many users said they were upset because their work depends on GitHub. Some were thankful for the honest explanation, but others wondered why GitHub waited for a big failure to fix these issues. There were warnings about relying too much on one platform and advice to keep backups or mirrors. Some liked the move to Azure, but others worried about being too dependent on Microsoft. Many talked about how hard it is to scale systems for millions of users, and shared their own outage stories. The community wants GitHub to be more reliable and communicate better during problems.

Next, we have a thoughtful article about the idea of “thickness” in art and writing. The author shares how he used to see classic art and books as boring, but changed his mind after learning to look deeper. “Thick” art is full of detail, history, and hidden meaning. It rewards those who pay attention and come back to it. He gives the example of Bosch’s “The Garden of Earthly Delights,” where even a small detail, like music notes written on a man’s butt, turns out to have a deeper reason. He says thick art asks the viewer or reader to do some work, like finding clues or thinking about choices. He warns that much modern content, including AI art, is “thin”—it looks real but has no real depth or history. He believes old, “thick” art is still valuable because it has survived for a reason.

In the comments, many agree that school ruined classics for them by not showing the value inside. Some say the best teachers helped them see deeper, while others only learned to enjoy classics later in life. Many like the idea that both creators and viewers need to put in effort for great art. Some say AI art is “thin” because it lacks real effort or history, though a few think new art can have “thickness” too. People share stories about finding meaning in art and discuss the “crack the egg” idea—the idea that being part of the process makes something more meaningful. There are some worries about elitism, but most agree that thickness is special and that shortcuts can’t replace it.

We also talk about the Consumer Rights Wiki, a website that collects information on anti-consumer practices and tools to help people know and defend their rights. The wiki is updated often, with new features like a feedback system, better privacy for anonymous editors, and more tools for moderators. It offers tools like ad blockers, scam checkers, recall databases, price trackers, and repair guides. Special projects collect consumer law from around the world and keep the site running smoothly.

Commenters praise the wiki as a useful central resource, especially with its global focus. Some worry about keeping information up to date and protecting the site from spam or vandalism. There’s support for the privacy changes, and suggestions to work with legal experts. Some users share their own bad experiences with companies and say they wish they had this kind of resource earlier. Questions about funding and neutrality are raised, but most agree the wiki is a good idea.

Another story reflects on how biology is taught in schools. The writer loved the subject in theory but found classes boring because they were focused on memorizing facts rather than exploring big questions and stories. He gives examples of how teaching the history and mysteries of biology, like Avery’s DNA experiment, could make lessons more interesting. He compares biology to programming, saying both are complex but programming is often taught using real projects, which helps students care more. He suggests that asking real questions and using stories, visuals, and hands-on labs can make biology more exciting.

Many commenters agree, saying they found biology boring in school but learned to enjoy it later through self-study or real-world connections. Some note that teaching through stories takes more time and training. There’s discussion about using technology to help, but also warnings not to forget the need for good teaching and curiosity. Most people agree that schools should help students see the wonder in biology and science.

We also look at Huzzah, a new tool for coding with AI. The author was tired of writing long English prompts to AI tools and wanted something better. Huzzah lets you write pseudocode as prompts, which are short, clear, and saved with the project. The tool turns this pseudocode into real code, and you can update your code by changing the pseudocode. This acts as both a prompt and documentation.

The idea has fans, who say writing pseudocode is natural and helps make your intent clear. Some worry that the AI may not always understand, or that big projects could be hard to handle. Others like that pseudocode could help when onboarding new team members. Some wish for more examples for larger projects, and a few still prefer manual coding for full control. Most are interested to see how Huzzah develops.

A technical issue came up with AliExpress, which uses hidden scripts on its homepage that process silent audio in your browser. This breaks Bluetooth multipoint on headphones, stopping audio from your phone when AliExpress is open on your PC. The scripts, part of Alibaba's anti-fraud tools, create invisible Web Audio contexts that keep the audio path active, even with no sound. They also collect lots of data to fingerprint your browser. Blocking these scripts with uBlock Origin stops the problem but may lead to more CAPTCHAs.

Commenters are unhappy with the hidden fingerprinting and surprised that silent audio can break hardware features. Many call for browsers to give users more control over background audio and fingerprinting. Some worry about the amount of tracking on the web, while others share tips on blocking similar scripts. There is hope that browsers will soon warn about or block this kind of activity. Most people agree that more transparency is needed from big sites.

We also look at new features in HTML that allow developers to do things without JavaScript. Now you can use attributes like `popover`, `<dialog>`, grouped `<details>`, `loading="lazy"`, and `hidden="until-found"` to create popups, dialogs, accordions, and more, all in plain HTML. There are built-in color pickers, range sliders, and date pickers too. The `<datalist>` tag creates autocomplete fields. The author advises checking browser support and accessibility, as some features still have bugs or work differently across browsers.

In the comments, many developers are excited about these changes, saying they make web development simpler and reduce the need for JavaScript. Some are careful, noting that support is not perfect and fallbacks may still be needed. There are a few bugs and accessibility issues, but most are hopeful that browser makers will improve things and that these features will become standard.

Next, we discuss the case of Aaron Swartz, who helped create RSS and was punished harshly for downloading academic articles, while companies like Meta copy huge amounts of books for AI with little penalty. Swartz faced up to 35 years in jail and a $1 million fine, and the stress led to his death. Meta downloaded 80 terabytes of books for AI, faces lawsuits, but likely only small fines. The author and many commenters feel this is unfair and shows that big companies are treated differently than regular people. Some defend copyright, but most agree the law is not applied equally and needs to change. There is sadness about Swartz’s story and frustration that so little has changed.

A major security story comes from the Rust world. The popular `arrayref` package was hacked after the maintainer’s account was taken over. A bad version added a fake dependency, `proc-macro1`, which downloaded and ran malware when anyone built their project. The attacker made old safe versions unusable, so users would upgrade to the hacked version. The malicious code used tricks to hide itself and worked on both Unix and Windows. With 245 million downloads and deep usage in other projects, the risk was very high.

Developers in the comments are worried. Some say this shows the risk of using open source code without checking all dependencies. There are calls for package systems to improve security, like not running build scripts without warnings and requiring stronger checks for popular packages. Some say the attack was clever, especially the trick of yanking old versions. Others ask for better tools and review systems, but many admit it’s hard to check everything. There is agreement that open source security needs more attention from everyone.

Finally, Linux 7.2 is out, after a busy development cycle. Key changes include cache-aware scheduling, better memory handling with MGLRU, new scheduler ideas with sched_ext, and better huge page support. A new fair GPU scheduler is included but not turned on by default due to a late bug. There is new runtime power management for Raspberry Pi 4 and 5 GPUs, and a 14-year-old bug in futex has been fixed. Other fixes cover USB drivers and memory checks at startup. There is progress toward full HDMI 2.1 support.

Comments show people are happy with the new Raspberry Pi features and the GPU scheduler. Some wish the fair scheduler was default, but understand the caution. Many are glad to see old bugs fixed and thank contributors like Igalia. There is talk about the growing size of the Linux kernel and the need for more testing. Raspberry Pi users hope the new fixes help their devices. There is also appreciation for better documentation and testing in the community. Overall, this release is seen as another strong step forward for Linux.
