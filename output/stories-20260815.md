So, being able to hold and use more information in your mind really matters for math. AI's big advantage is that it can remember dozens or hundreds of facts, equations, and steps at once, while humans cannot.

The article says this memory boost helps most with math because math is made up of clear symbols and rules. In other subjects, like talking about why someone is upset, having more memory does not always help, because the real reason may be hidden or unclear. So, the AI's memory power is most useful in math, where you can write everything out and check your work.

AI does not keep private thoughts like people do. Instead, it "thinks out loud" by writing down every step. This is actually very helpful in math, since you want every step to be clear and correct. The writer also notes that AI is not the same as a genius like Einstein, who found totally new ways to look at problems. Instead, AI is more like someone who is very fast, organized, and never forgets anything.

Some readers say that AI still makes silly mistakes or misses the main idea of a problem, which shows it is not truly understanding like a person. A few people worry that if AI can do all the "bookkeeping," it might make human mathematicians lazy or less careful.

A number of commenters enjoy the comparison between Einstein and von Neumann, saying it fits: AI is like a super-fast, organized thinker, but not yet a deep or original one. Some hope that in the future, AI will not just follow rules but will find new ways of thinking about math.

One commenter notes that for some hard problems, the key step is a creative leap, and AI is not good at that yet. Others argue that AI’s ability to check every case and remember every rule is already changing math, making it easier to tackle long, complicated proofs.

A few people wonder if giving humans better tools—like smarter notebooks or better software—could help us keep up with AI. Some believe that as AI gets better, the real skill for humans will be to ask the right questions or to see things in a new way.

Overall, most agree that AI’s big advantage in math comes from its huge memory, not magical intelligence, and that we should be careful when comparing it to people.

## At-home test for infected ticks could improve Lyme Disease diagnosis

- 原文链接: [At-home test for infected ticks could improve Lyme Disease diagnosis](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/the-first-at-home-test-for-infected-ticks-could-improve-lyme-disease-diagnosis-180989235/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49310682)

A new at-home test called LymeAlert will soon let people check if a tick that bit them carries Lyme disease. This comes as tick bites and tick-borne illnesses are rising in the U.S., with Lyme disease being the most common and cases getting worse in areas like the Northeast and Midwest.

The article explains that most tick bites are harmless, but some ticks spread serious diseases. Quick treatment is important because Lyme disease is easiest to cure early, but getting a fast and clear diagnosis is hard. Many doctors prescribe antibiotics just in case, which can lead to problems like antibiotic resistance.

LymeAlert is a simple kit: you remove the tick, crush it in the kit, add a special liquid, and use a test strip—like a pregnancy or COVID test. In about 15 minutes, you can see if the tick had Lyme bacteria. There’s an app too, which helps read results and can connect you with a doctor if needed. This is faster than mailing ticks to a lab, which often takes too long for quick treatment. Current blood tests for Lyme disease often miss early cases, so this home test could help people act fast.

Ticks are spreading more because of warmer winters and more deer and mice, their favorite hosts. Only a few tick types spread most diseases, but their range is growing. Managing ticks is hard since they don’t travel far by themselves and live close to their animal hosts, so most prevention is left to individuals.

Experts in the article say the at-home test is useful, but not perfect. The CDC warns that false results can happen, and a positive tick doesn’t mean you’ll get sick—only a small percent of bites lead to Lyme disease. Still, knowing if a tick carried Lyme can give people peace of mind and help doctors decide on treatment. The LymeAlert team is also working on maps to show tick hot spots, using data from the app and vets.

In the Hacker News comments, some users are excited about the test, saying it could save time and worry for families, especially in high-risk areas. Others point out the limits: a positive test doesn’t guarantee infection, and a negative might give false comfort. Several users mention the risk of overusing antibiotics if people rely only on these tests. Some worry about false negatives missing real danger, while others think the test is a good extra tool if people understand it’s not perfect. A few commenters suggest more focus on prevention, like tick checks and habitat changes, rather than only relying on testing. There’s also debate about the $50 cost—some think it’s fair for peace of mind, while others say it should be cheaper or covered by insurance. Finally, many agree that better public awareness and education about ticks and Lyme disease are still very important.

## RISC-V: They Should Have Known Better

- 原文链接: [RISC-V: They Should Have Known Better](https://dmitry.gr/?r=06.%20Thoughts&proj=12.%20RV)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49298035)

This article is about the problems the author sees in the RISC-V CPU architecture. The author thinks RISC-V is not as good as people say, and points out many design issues.

First, the author explains that RISC-V tries to be good for every use, from tiny microcontrollers to big servers, but this is impossible. Small microcontrollers need fast interrupts and small code, but RISC-V handles interrupts slower and less efficiently than ARM Cortex-M0. The compressed instructions in RISC-V do not help much, and sometimes make things worse. For big server CPUs, RISC-V’s instruction length and design also cause problems, like making it hard to quickly decode instructions and handle common operations such as array access. The author complains that useful features are missing or were added too late, and the way instructions are encoded is confusing and inconsistent.

A major issue is that too many features in RISC-V are optional. This means different chips may support different sets of instructions, making it hard for software to work everywhere. For example, even important things like multiplication or handling interrupts are not always present. There is no simple way for software to check what the chip supports, because the registers used for this are also optional or may give wrong answers. The system timer and interrupt handling are also inconsistent, which makes writing software for all RISC-V chips very hard.

The design of RISC-V instructions is also criticized. The way numbers (immediates) are encoded in instructions seems random and makes emulation and decoding harder. There are many instruction formats, and some instructions use bits in strange ways, making things confusing. If two chips support different sets of extensions, the same instruction could mean something different, which can cause software to break in strange ways.

The author says that to fix these problems, the RISC-V group made “profiles” that tell which optional features should be included for certain uses, but most current RISC-V boards do not follow these profiles. This means many popular boards will not run future versions of Linux or Android. The only Linux version that can handle this situation well is Gentoo, because it builds software for each chip.

The author thinks RISC-V will become popular for very cheap devices, not because it is good, but because it is free to use. For high-performance uses or desktops, the author believes ARM and x86 are still better because their designs are more complete and stable.

In the comments, many people agree that RISC-V’s optional features make things messy, especially for software developers who want their code to run everywhere. Some say the problems come from trying to please too many people and making the base set too small. Others think the open nature of RISC-V is still a big advantage, and these issues can be fixed over time.

Some readers defend the RISC-V design, saying it is still new and will improve, while others point out that ARM and x86 also had problems when they started. A few commenters say the optional extensions are needed to let companies build only what they need and keep chips simple or cheap. There are also comments about academia’s influence, with some agreeing that the design feels “too academic” and not practical.

A few people mention that the instruction encoding is not as bad as the author says, and that hardware can handle the complexity. Others worry about future software compatibility and think the confusion will hurt RISC-V’s reputation. Some hope that as the market grows, RISC-V will choose better defaults and become easier to use, but others are less optimistic and think the early mistakes will be hard to fix.

In the 1970s and 80s, his research made it possible to switch from analog to digital systems. This change is why we can now stream music and video, use smartphones, and communicate over the internet so easily. He worked to make digital processing faster and use less power, which was important for devices like cellphones. Liu wrote a well-known textbook on DSP and published over 250 research papers. He also received 12 patents, including one for a method that made signal processing much more efficient. Besides his research, Liu was a mentor to many students, some of whom later became leaders at companies like Google and IBM. One of his students, Robert Kahn, helped create the internet’s architecture.

Later in his career, Liu worked on protecting digital media from copying and tampering. He helped develop digital watermarks to show if images or videos were real or fake. He was also active in professional groups, serving on the IEEE Board of Directors and being honored with several awards. Outside of work, people remember him as friendly, cheerful, and encouraging, always ready with advice or a smile.

## Abdominal Fat Predicts Heart Disease Risk Better Than BMI

- 原文链接: [Abdominal Fat Predicts Heart Disease Risk Better Than BMI](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2026/08/11/14/59/abdominal-fat-predicts-heart-disease-risk-better-than-bmi)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49314403)

A new study says that fat around your stomach is a better way to tell if you might get heart disease than using BMI. BMI is often used to check if someone is overweight, but it does not show where your body fat is.

The study looked at over 260,000 people for 20 years. It checked their waist size (waist circumference, or WC) and the ratio of their waist to their hips (waist-to-hip ratio, or WHR) along with their BMI. The results showed that many people who had a normal BMI still had a lot of fat around their stomach. These people had a higher chance of heart problems. Some people with overweight or obesity had less belly fat and did not have as high a risk.

Fat inside your belly, called visceral fat, is worse for your heart than fat under your skin. The study found that people with a normal BMI but high belly fat had a 15% to 50% higher risk of heart attack, stroke, or dying early. People with obesity but not much belly fat had a lower risk than expected.

Doctors in the study now say that just looking at BMI is not enough. Measuring your waist or checking your waist-to-hip ratio gives a better idea of your risk. The study did not look at things like exercise, diet, or genes, which can also change your risk.

In the comments, many people agree that BMI can be misleading, especially for people who have a lot of muscle or different body types. Some say that belly fat is easy to measure at home, so it is practical. Others think doctors should already be using waist size, but many still rely on BMI because it’s fast and simple. A few people share their own stories, saying their BMI is high, but they are healthy and active, while others with normal BMI have health problems. Some worry that focusing on belly fat might make people feel bad about their bodies. Others point out that lifestyle, like food and exercise, matters too—not just the numbers. A few ask for more research, especially about how things change over time. Overall, most people like the idea of using more than just BMI to check heart risk.

## A Spectre Is Haunting Unicode

- 原文链接: [A Spectre Is Haunting Unicode](https://www.dampfkraft.com/ghost-characters.html)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49310926)

This article is about strange “ghost characters” that appeared in a Japanese character encoding standard from 1978. These characters had no clear meaning, source, or pronunciation, and even experts didn’t know where they came from.

The standard, known as JIS X 0208, tried to include all important Japanese characters, especially those used in place names. But after it was published, people noticed a few characters that seemed to have no history. Researchers later called these “ghost characters.” In 1997, an investigation tried to track down the origins of each character. The process was hard because the “sources” were often just huge books with thousands of pages, only listing the document, not the exact page. Some ghost characters were found to be simple cataloging mistakes. For example, one character was created when two separate pieces of paper were pasted together, and a new line was accidentally added where the papers joined. Another ghost character probably came from someone misreading a similar-looking character. Despite being errors, these ghost characters were added into the official standard and, later, into Unicode. This means they now exist on almost every computer in the world, even though no one uses them or knows what they mean.

In the comments, several people were surprised and amused that simple mistakes could become part of a global computer standard forever. Some found the story funny, while others saw it as a warning about how easy it is for errors to spread when building big systems. A few commenters shared similar stories from other countries or languages, where “junk” or accidental data became part of official records. Some people wondered if we should try to remove these ghost characters from Unicode, but others argued it’s better to keep them for historical reasons. One person pointed out that these ghosts show how complicated and messy language and technology can be. Others liked the idea that there are now “mystery” characters hidden inside their computers. A couple of users joked that maybe these ghosts are like digital “Easter eggs,” making the story of Unicode more interesting. Some developers said this is a good example of why standards and documentation are important, and why we should always check our sources carefully. A few mentioned the technical challenges of dealing with rare and unused characters in programming. Overall, the comments showed a mix of surprise, humor, and respect for the weird ways technology and history can mix together.
