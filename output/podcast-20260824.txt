First, Xiaomi just released a new CPU for their phones, called the Xring O3. This chip is as fast as Apple’s best chip in single tasks, and even faster when doing many things at once. One special thing is its huge 44 MB cache memory—more than many laptop CPUs, even some from Intel. The most powerful core, the C1-Ultra, supports SME2 for AI work and SVE2 for working on lots of data at the same time. The core is very wide, with 21 execution ports, and 6 of those can do SIMD, which means doing lots of similar math at the same time. For ARM chips, this is the best right now. In Geekbench tests, it scored 3,945 for single-core and 15,221 for multi-core, which are very high numbers for a phone.

Hacker News users are excited but also a bit unsure. Some think the chip sounds amazing but want to see real proof. One user points out that other chips with C1-Ultra did not get such high scores. People are also impressed by the 21 execution ports and the large cache, saying it shows phone CPUs are getting more like desktop CPUs. But some worry the numbers might not be real, or could change in real phones. Others compare Xiaomi to Apple, wondering how long Xiaomi will keep the lead. Overall, people are interested and waiting to see more tests.

Next, there is an unusual story from China. A group online has started saying the famous Tang Dynasty never happened. The rumor began when a history influencer used strange astrology to claim the Tang Dynasty was fake. Some people even called tourist offices in Xi’an, the old Tang capital, asking them to close. The hashtag “the Tang dynasty doesn’t exist” became very popular on Chinese social media.

Experts say this is nonsense. The Tang Dynasty is a proven fact and was very important for China and the region. As the rumor grew, Chinese media and government stepped in, calling the claim dangerous and warning it could hurt national pride. The government calls this kind of rumor “historical nihilism” and punishes people for questioning official history. Some historians say people talk about the past to discuss today’s problems or to rebel. The government blocked the influencer’s accounts and removed videos. Now, anyone searching about the Tang Dynasty will mostly see posts saying the rumor is false.

On Hacker News, many users call the hoax silly and compare it to conspiracy theories in other countries. Some think it shows how quickly fake news spreads online. Others say it is interesting how history can be used to push ideas or rebel against authority. A few feel sorry for young people in China who may now be confused. Some joke about what else people might start denying. Others warn that blocking people just makes rumors more interesting to some. Most agree the story is funny, but also sad, because it shows how easy it is for people to doubt even basic facts.

Moving on, Microsoft Paint and Photos now add an invisible watermark to images made with their AI tools, even if you make them on your own computer. The way it works is that before making an image, your prompt is sent to Microsoft’s server for a safety check. The server sends back a special code called a GUID, which is hidden in the image pixels by a tool called Watermarker.dll. You cannot turn this hidden watermark off, and if it cannot be added, Paint will not give you the image. The Photos app also adds the watermark, but if it fails, you still get the image. The GUID links your image to the prompt you used, and extra information is also added using the C2PA standard.

Even if generation is local, you still need an internet connection and a Microsoft account, because the watermark comes from the server. You can only save AI images in formats that keep this extra data, like PNG or JPEG.

Some Hacker News users are surprised and worried about privacy, noting that people may not expect a local image to get a remote-unique ID. Others think this is needed to follow new AI rules, like those in the EU. Some feel this is not real “local” generation at all. A few say watermarking is good for stopping abuse, but others worry it could be used to track users. Some suggest using open tools like Stable Diffusion to avoid these watermarks. Others wonder if Microsoft should be clearer about what happens with your data. Most people agree power users should be told, and some ask if this could hurt trust in Microsoft’s AI tools.

Our next story is about a website where you can “walk” around a digital version of San Francisco, almost like a video game. You use keyboard keys and your mouse to move, and can jump, run, use a glider, or drive a vehicle. The map is built from real-world data, with street layouts and buildings. You can teleport to different places or zoom in and out. The project loads parts of the city as you move, and shows info about your position. It uses Apple Maps data for graphics and layout, and is still a work in progress.

Hacker News commenters were impressed by the detail and enjoyed exploring familiar places. Some liked how easy it was to use, and thought it could be good for planning trips or just for fun. Others discussed the technical side, like how map tiles load and how it compares to Google Earth or Microsoft Flight Simulator. Some worried about copyright, since it uses Apple Maps data. There were jokes about using it to avoid real San Francisco problems, and suggestions for new features like adding more cities or multiplayer. Some found the website slow or buggy, especially on older computers. Others saw educational uses for virtual tours. Most agreed it was a fun and creative way to see San Francisco online.

Now to the oceans. The world’s oceans have reached their hottest temperature ever, an average of 21.1°C. This new record is linked to both climate change and a growing El Niño event. Usually, oceans are hottest in March or April, but this record happened in August, which is strange. Most of the extra heat from greenhouse gases goes into the oceans—over 90%. Warmer oceans can cause stronger storms, damage sea life like coral reefs, and raise sea levels. Some places, such as the western English Channel, have had marine heatwaves for more than three years. Scientists are worried because El Niño is still growing, and the temperature could go even higher by the end of the year. Warmer sea water also means more flooding and extreme weather.

Hacker News users share different views. Some are shocked and worried, saying this shows climate change is getting worse. A few ask if the data is correct, but most agree the trend is real. Others talk about how warmer oceans will hurt fishing and tourism, and say we need to cut fossil fuel use and develop cleaner energy. Some feel hopeless, while others say even small actions can help. A few share ideas for cooling the oceans, but most agree solutions will be hard and need global effort.

Next, Shipyard, a team working on the IPFS project, announced they are stopping their work because Protocol Labs will not fund them anymore. Their last day is September 30, 2026. Shipyard made websites and downloads easier to use in browsers, made the IPFS gateway faster and cheaper, and worked on using IPFS with HTTP. They also helped keep popular IPFS tools and public services running. They wanted to keep improving IPFS, but now they cannot finish their projects. Projects like Kubo, Helia, and IPFS Desktop will lose their main maintainers, and public services like ipfs.io may also shut down or change.

In the comments, people are sad and worried. Some say this is a big loss for the IPFS community. Others wonder if IPFS has funding problems or is too hard to keep running. Some hope new people or groups will step in, and a few discuss technical issues like slow speeds. Some say this shows the risk of depending on one group for important tools. Others thank Shipyard for their work, and some think maybe the community will step up. Most feel sad but want to see what happens next.

Another story talks about new packaging rules in the European Union. These rules are making it very hard for small makers and micro-businesses to sell products across EU countries. The EU wants to cut packaging waste, which is good, but the rules are very complex. Every small seller has to register and pay fees in every EU country where they have customers. For people who only sell a few things, the cost is huge—over €1,000 a year for just ten sales in four countries. Most small sellers cannot afford this, and if nothing changes, they may stop selling in the EU.

The article suggests three solutions: let small sellers with low sales skip the rules, make a single EU website for all countries, or let platforms handle the rules for their sellers. Many agree that the rules are too hard for small businesses. Some say the EU does not think about small sellers, and the rules only help big companies. Others agree that cutting waste is important, but the rules are not fair. Some worry other regions like the US may copy these rules. Most agree that something needs to change so small makers can survive.

Next, NIST, a U.S. agency, is selling peanut butter for over $1,200 for three jars—about $415 each. This is not normal peanut butter. It is a Standard Reference Material, or SRM, used by labs to check their equipment and test methods. The peanut butter is measured very carefully, so labs know exactly what is inside. The high price covers the testing, packing, and paperwork. Labs, not normal people, buy these SRMs. In the comments, people joke about the price, but others explain why it costs so much. Labs need exact results, and for them, the cost is worth it. Some remember using NIST peanut butter in school. Others compare the price to other expensive lab items. Most agree it is not for lunch—it is for science.

Another article looks back at early AI software from the 1970s to 1990s, now collected by the Internet Archive. Programs like ELIZA acted like a psychologist, and others like Racter wrote strange stories. Some games let you guide virtual people or program simple robots. These old programs were simple, but made people feel like computers could think. The new “Vintage Artificial Intelligence” collection lets people see and use these programs online.

In the comments, some people are nostalgic about these early programs. Others say people have always wanted to believe machines are smarter than they are. Some discuss how these programs got them interested in programming and AI. A few say early AI was more fun and less scary than today’s systems. Many like that the Internet Archive saves these programs, so new generations can see how AI ideas started. Some share their own projects inspired by these old systems. The comments show a mix of fond memories and thoughts about how our ideas of AI have changed.

Our last story is about the importance of owning our digital communication tools, using Jabber/XMPP as an example. The writer says digital services like messaging apps should be seen as basic infrastructure. Open standards like XMPP help people switch tools or providers and avoid being locked in by one company. XMPP is managed by a group, and many different apps and servers use it. Over the years, XMPP has added new features and is still active today.

Some commenters say XMPP is still too hard for most people, with confusing setup and not enough polish. Others say things have improved, with apps like Conversations and Dino now easy to use. Some wish XMPP was more popular, but think it lost ground when mobile apps became common. Others think governments should use XMPP to avoid being locked in. Some warn that open standards are only useful if enough people use them. Overall, the comments show hope that XMPP can still play a big role in the future of messaging.
