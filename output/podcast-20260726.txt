Let’s start with Decker, a creative tool inspired by old classics like HyperCard and classic MacOS. Decker lets you make interactive documents with images, sound, links, and even code. You can use it in your browser or as a native app for Windows, Mac, Linux, and BSD. The goal is to keep things simple and creative—Decker uses only black and white pixel art, helping you focus on ideas instead of too many style choices. It comes with modern features: undo history, touch and scroll support, keyboard shortcuts, and ways to edit many items at once. You can make e-zines, notes, slides, games, or just have fun doodling. Projects are saved as self-contained HTML files, ready to share anywhere.

For those who want to go deeper, Decker has its own scripting language called Lil. Lil is simple but powerful, inspired by Lua and Q, and can handle lists, queries, and even custom widgets. You can use Lil scripts both in Decker and from the command line with a tool called Lilt. Decker files are just text, so they work well with Git or SVN. There are no ads or tracking, and it’s free and open source under the MIT license. The community is active on Itch.io, and there are guides and examples to help you get started.

In the comments, many people feel nostalgic about HyperCard and are happy to see something like it again. Users praise the simple black-and-white design, saying it boosts creativity and is great for beginners. Some love the Lil scripting language, though a few wish it was closer to other popular languages. The choice to save projects as HTML makes sharing easy, and many see Decker as future-proof. Some users ask about more colors, embedding decks into bigger web projects, or using Decker in schools. Overall, the mood is positive, with many excited to try Decker and recommend it to friends.

Another article discusses compromise in design. The author says that every design is a set of tradeoffs—choosing what to focus on and what to let go. Companies often claim their products have “no compromises,” but this is never true. Good products are opinionated and choose to be great at some things, even if it means being worse at others. If you try to please everyone, you end up with a product that pleases no one.

In the comments, many agree that compromise is part of every design. Some say clear tradeoffs make products better and more honest. A few disagree, saying that sometimes wide appeal is needed for business. Overall, most agree: picking the right tradeoffs is what makes a product good or bad.

Now, let’s look at Data-Oriented Design, or DOD, which is popular in game development. DOD focuses on how data moves in memory, not just how code is organized. In Object-Oriented Design (OOD), data for each object is mixed, which can be slow when the CPU tries to read or write. DOD puts similar data together in arrays, making updates faster and more friendly for modern hardware like GPUs.

The comments show that DOD brings big speed gains, especially for games and real-time apps. Some say OOD is still better for large, complex projects where maintainability matters more than speed. Many agree that a mixed approach is often best—use DOD for hot code paths, OOD for high-level logic. There are also questions about how DOD fits with modern languages, and some warn that DOD can be harder for new team members to understand. The general feeling: there’s no one right way—choose what fits your project’s needs.

Let’s move on to CheapSecurity, an open-source CCTV app for Linux single-board computers like Raspberry Pi. It helps you build a private, affordable security camera system using USB webcams and your own hardware. All video is stored locally—nothing goes to the cloud—giving you full control over your data. The app is lightweight, supports many cameras, and is easy to install with Python and OpenCV.

Features include live streaming, motion detection, email and Telegram alerts, night mode, and easy management of old recordings. The dashboard runs in your browser and lets you watch video, change settings, and handle files. The code is under the AGPLv3 license, and you’re advised to be careful with remote access for safety.

Commenters like the privacy focus and how easy it is to use spare hardware. Some share tips on running it on different boards or setting up cameras. There are worries about security and advice to use strong passwords and careful network setups. Some ask for more features, like multi-camera support. Others compare it to similar projects and say CheapSecurity is easier to set up. Overall, people see it as a practical, private way to build a home security system without extra fees or losing control of your data.

Another important story explains how a hidden market uses “relay” services to resell AI tokens at low prices, helping people avoid official limits and sometimes enabling fraud. These relays act as middlemen, selling access to AI services at huge discounts. The system uses open-source tools like “one-api” and “new-api,” which are legal, but problems start when stolen or abused tokens are used.

Fraudsters use tricks like making fake accounts, charging back after using services, or using open chatbots as hidden relays. This market is now very big, with websites comparing prices and even lotteries for free API keys. AI companies try to fight back with stricter account checks and rules, but there is no perfect solution.

Commenters are shocked at how big and mature this market is. Some say it’s an arms race between providers and abusers. Others worry that strong anti-fraud rules might hurt honest users, especially in strict countries. There’s debate about whether using relays is always wrong—some see it as a gray market. Most agree that the cat-and-mouse game will continue, and there’s no simple fix.

Switching gears, we have an article about writing good English prose. The author compares rich, complex writing styles with plain, clear ones, and says the best writing balances both. English prose became strong because the language borrowed words from many places. The author warns against following strict writing rules, like “always use the active voice” or “never use long words,” arguing that these can make writing boring.

Instead, he suggests using the best word for the job, reading widely, and not being afraid to surprise readers. The author dislikes rules from books like "The Elements of Style," saying they are too limiting. He also says to read your writing out loud to check its flow.

In the comments, many agree that modern writing is too simple and dull. Some defend simple writing, saying it can be beautiful when done well. Others note that English is rich because it borrows words from many languages. There’s general agreement that good writing is about finding your own voice and making the words come alive, not just following rules.
