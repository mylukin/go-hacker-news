## How I find problems to solve as a staff engineer

- 原文链接: [How I find problems to solve as a staff engineer](https://lalitm.com/post/find-problems-staff-engineer/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49411643)

This article is about how a staff engineer finds important problems to solve, instead of just waiting for tasks from a manager. The writer explains their method for finding valuable work that really helps the team and organization.

The writer says that blocking out time to “think big” does not work well for them. Instead, they listen to what people complain about in meetings, chats, and emails. They let these problems sit in their mind and look for patterns over time. Sometimes, different complaints are really the same problem, and solving it can help many people.

They notice that many engineers just wait for managers to give them hard problems, but the biggest impact comes from finding and solving problems before leaders even see them. The writer works in big companies with teams that have freedom to choose what to work on, so this method fits their work style.

When people ask for a new feature, the writer asks questions to find the real problem behind the request. They don’t just build what is asked for, but try to deeply understand what slows people down. They talk to people from other teams and look for issues that show up in different places.

The writer learned not to rush to fix every problem right away. If they wait, sometimes the same problem appears in many teams, showing it is important. They keep track of problems and wait to see which ones matter most before starting new projects.

For example, many teams wanted small changes in a tool called Perfetto. After collecting requests, the writer realized people really wanted to customize the tool for their own work. So, they built a way to extend the tool, not just add single features.

Before building a big change, the writer tests the idea. They might make a small prototype, talk to more people, or write a proposal. If no one cares, they stop. If the idea is good but the timing is wrong, they wait. Sometimes, they give the idea to someone else to build.

The writer says that every time they solve a useful problem, people trust them more and bring more interesting problems. This creates a loop, making it easier to find and solve new problems.

In the comments, many people agree with the advice to listen and look for patterns, not just do what is asked. Some say it is harder to do this in companies with strict rules or less freedom for engineers. Others say that waiting before acting is smart, as people often ask for things they don’t really need. A few warn that it takes time to build trust, and not all managers will listen to staff engineers early on. Some share their own methods, like keeping a notebook of problems, or walking around to clear their mind and see connections. There are also comments about the risk of solving the wrong problem, and people agree that checking with others before building is important. Some readers ask how to use these ideas in smaller companies or as remote workers, and others stress the value of talking to people outside your own team. Overall, most agree that finding and shaping the right problem is a key skill for senior engineers.

## How Complex Systems Fail (1998)

- 原文链接: [How Complex Systems Fail (1998)](https://how.complexsystems.fail/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49409473)

The article explains why complex systems, like hospitals or power plants, often fail in surprising ways. It says these systems are always risky by their very nature, but we add many layers of protection to keep them working safely.

Failures still happen, though. Usually, it takes several small problems joining together to cause a big disaster—one mistake alone is not enough. These small issues are always there, hidden in the system, and the system keeps running because it has backups and because people work around the problems. After an accident, people often say the warning signs were clear, but that's easy to say once you know what happened. In truth, it’s hard to spot danger before something goes wrong.

Trying to find a single "root cause" for big failures isn't helpful, because accidents need many things to go wrong at once. It's not fair to blame only one person or problem. Human operators are both the people who keep things running and the ones who stop accidents. They have to make choices without knowing the outcome—every action is a gamble. Organizations often focus on getting work done, but after something breaks, they suddenly care more about safety.

Humans are important because they can adapt to changes and fix problems as they happen. But as technology and staff change, new types of mistakes appear, and it’s hard to see them before they cause trouble. Making systems safer is not just about fixing parts; safety depends on the whole system and the people in it, and it’s always changing. People keep the system safe by reacting to problems in real time. To really improve, operators need to understand what failure looks like and learn from it.

In the Hacker News comments, many users agree that complex systems can never be made completely safe. Some share stories from fields like aviation or software, saying accidents often come from many small issues lining up. Others note that blaming one person after something goes wrong is unfair and doesn’t help prevent future problems.

A few users point out that adding more rules or tools after an accident can actually make things more complicated and increase risk. Some say that learning from near-misses is just as important as learning from failures. One commenter highlights that people on the ground usually know about the risks, but management often ignores their warnings. Another user says it’s hard to balance getting work done with staying safe, especially under pressure.

There are also comments about how new technology can solve some problems but create new, unexpected ones. Some people wish companies would spend more time understanding how things really fail, not just fixing easy-to-see mistakes. A few mention how important it is for workers to have good training and experience with real problems. Others remind us that safety is never finished—it’s something people must work on every day.

## The Vibe Tax

- 原文链接: [The Vibe Tax](https://insufferable.dev/posts/vibe-tax/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49411199)

This article talks about the “Vibe Tax,” a hidden cost when using AI agents to help write code. The author wanted to build a simple to-do app with help from a large language model (LLM) named Pol, hoping it would save time and effort.

The author gave Pol careful instructions and left it to work overnight. But in the morning, they saw their entire weekly AI usage quota was gone, with nothing to show for it—no app, just a bunch of complex tests in empty folders. The LLM had spent all its resources making sure every tiny edge case was covered in tests, but never actually wrote the main app.

Digging deeper, the author realized this happened because many people use AI tools in a way that teaches them to over-engineer solutions. The AI learns to write code that’s super safe and very complete, but also very expensive and sometimes useless. It creates lots of tests and covers cases that almost never happen, burning through lots of tokens (the units you pay for with AIs). The result is that one person’s “vibe coding”—letting the AI do whatever, without checking—makes things more expensive for everyone. This is the “vibe tax” that all users pay.

In the comments, some people agreed and said they had similar experiences where AI tools wasted resources or created too much unneeded code. Others pointed out that over-engineering has always been a problem in software, even before AI, and that humans do it too. A few commenters thought the author should have set better limits or clearer tasks for the AI, to avoid burning through the quota. Some said that as AIs get better, they might waste less, but for now, you need to watch them closely. A couple of readers joked that maybe the real “vibe tax” is paying for all the tests nobody will ever use. Others wondered if AI companies would change how they charge for usage, to avoid this problem. Some developers liked having lots of tests, but agreed it shouldn’t come at the cost of missing the main goal. Overall, the discussion showed that many people are struggling with how to use AI tools well, and that the “vibe tax” is a real concern for today’s software engineers.

## What Is a Harness?

- 原文链接: [What Is a Harness?](https://earendil.com/posts/what-is-a-harness/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49409092)

This article explains what a harness is, first in climbing, then in artificial intelligence. In climbing, a harness keeps you safe by connecting you to ropes and lets you carry tools; you can adjust it for different climbs.

An agent harness in AI is similar: it is software that gives an AI model a safe and controlled space to work. The harness gives the AI a set of instructions (called a system prompt), lets it use tools (like searching the web or writing code), and manages how the AI makes decisions and repeats actions (this is called the agentic loop). For example, if you ask an AI agent in an email harness to research schools, it can search the web, collect data, make a spreadsheet, and write you an email—all by itself, repeating steps as needed until the job is done.

A key part of the harness is the translation layer. This lets your harness connect to different AI models, like ones from OpenAI or Anthropic, or open-source models. This means you are not locked in; you can choose the best or cheapest model for each task. The article says this gives users more power and freedom, because you can run your own harness on your own computer, keep your data private, and adapt the harness to your needs—just like people customize climbing harnesses.

Some harnesses, like Pi, are open source and easy to extend. Users share thousands of extensions and can change how the harness works. The author thinks this is important because it makes people less dependent on big AI companies and gives them more control.

In the comments, some readers say the article is a clear explanation of a confusing topic and thank the author for the simple language. Others argue that the term "harness" is still a bit vague and overlaps with words like "framework" or "orchestrator." A few developers share their experience making their own harnesses and mention both the fun and the complexity involved.

Some users think open source harnesses are the future, giving people more independence and privacy. Others worry that most users will still choose commercial apps for convenience, ignoring the benefits of owning their own harness. A few people point out that running your own harness needs technical skills, which could limit who can use them.

There are also debates about whether agent harnesses really protect privacy, since most rely on cloud AI models for now. Some commenters say they like the idea of comparing different AI models inside one harness, especially for work or research. Others hope that more open source harnesses will make it easier for everyone to use AI safely and flexibly.

## Why Sal Khan't: On Learning by Making but Teaching by Telling

- 原文链接: [Why Sal Khan't: On Learning by Making but Teaching by Telling](https://punyamishra.com/2026/04/16/why-sal-khant-on-learning-by-making-but-teaching-by-telling/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49409862)

This article talks about why Khanmigo, the AI tutor from Sal Khan (of Khan Academy), did not change education like some people hoped. The writer says that even though Sal Khan learned by exploring, asking questions, and making things, his tool just told students information or explained things to them.

The article gives examples of how Sal Khan learns new things for himself. He reads, maps ideas, asks deep questions, talks to experts, and tries to build his own understanding. He then makes videos to share what he learned. This process is active and personal—he is always building, questioning, and making connections. But when it comes to what students do in Khan Academy, they only watch videos or talk to a chatbot. They do not get to explore, make, or create anything themselves. The writer says learning happens when people have a reason to care, ask questions, and try to make something, not just watch or listen.

The article also talks about old ideas from John Dewey, who said real learning comes from four things: asking questions, making things, expressing ideas, and sharing with others. Sal Khan did all of these when he learned. But students using Khan Academy or Khanmigo do not get to do these things; they just get the result of someone else’s learning journey. The writer says this is why students do not care much about the AI tutor. Watching someone else’s learning is not the same as learning yourself.

At the end, the article says technology companies keep thinking that giving the right content in the right way will make people learn. But without giving students a purpose or letting them explore and make things, real learning does not happen. Teachers know how to do this, but videos and chatbots do not.

In the comments on Hacker News, some people agree and say that learning by doing is much more powerful than just being told information. They talk about how they remember things better when they try to use or build with the ideas. Others point out that not everyone learns the same way—some people do like videos and explanations, especially if they do not know where to start. A few people defend Khan Academy, saying it helps a lot of people who would not have a teacher or guide otherwise. Some wonder if the problem is not the tool, but how it is used in schools. Others say that AI tutors could get better in the future and learn to ask questions or give challenges, not just explain. There are also comments about how hard it is to motivate students, and that no tool can fix that alone. A few teachers in the comments say they use videos and chatbots as just one part of their teaching, not everything. Some people wish tech companies would listen more to teachers and students when building new tools. Overall, the comments show that people have many ideas about how learning works and what technology can—and can’t—do to help.

## Malware infects Android-based automotive head unit firmware

- 原文链接: [Malware infects Android-based automotive head unit firmware](https://securelist.com/android-head-unit-malware/121106/)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49408550)

This article is about new malware found on Android-based car head units, which are the screens and systems in cars that often run Android. The malware spreads using the head unit’s official software updater, so many users do not know their devices are infected.

The malware works in several steps. First, a real app called TWCore, which is used for system updates and gathering data, downloads an app called JarService. JarService has no user interface and only acts as a downloader for the next part of the malware. Next, JarService decrypts and loads another hidden app, which then contacts the attacker’s servers and gets another download link. The new downloaded payload collects information about the device and regularly checks for new commands and updates from the attacker’s servers. The malware can do many things, like show ads, commit ad fraud (fake clicks), and download even more code. One of the final stages is to turn the car head unit into a part of a proxy botnet, where it can be used for internet traffic by others, often for illegal activity.

Researchers found that this attack was done by a group called MoYu, which is linked to other large botnets like BADBOX. The group used the official update system of the head unit, so the infection could spread very quickly and widely. They also found similar attacks on Android TV boxes, not just cars. The malware is clever, as it hides well and can update itself and its tasks remotely. The car head unit vendor was told about the problem and said they fixed the security issues.

People in the comments are shocked that car head units can get infected this way. Some say it’s a big problem that car makers use Android but do not keep it updated or secure. Others talk about how cars are now like computers, but with much less security. A few users are worried that if attackers can get into the car system, maybe they could control more important car functions in the future. Some think the main risk is not stealing data, but using these devices for botnets and ad fraud. There are also comments saying that car owners should be able to update or check their own devices for malware, but most people don’t know how. Some users say this is a warning for all smart devices, not just cars. Others point out that most people buy these head units as cheap upgrades and may get them from untrusted sellers. A few are glad that security researchers found the problem and told the vendor. Finally, some worry that these kinds of attacks will only get worse as more devices run Android or other open systems.

## My agent.md to improve LLM-assisted code quality

- 原文链接: [My agent.md to improve LLM-assisted code quality](https://fabiensanglard.net/agent.md/index.html)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49410932)

This article explains how the author improved code quality when using AI code assistants, like LLMs, for programming. At first, the code generated by these tools was messy and hard to use, even if it sometimes worked or found bugs.

The main problem was poor code structure, missing comments, and confusing logic. The author tried agentic IDEs like Antigravity and Claude Code in VS Code, which allowed them to guide the AI step by step. The code quality got much better, but the author had to repeat the same feedback each time, like “don’t use magic numbers” or “add comments.” To solve this, the author created a file called agent.md. This file holds all their preferred coding rules and suggestions. When a coding session starts, the IDE loads agent.md so the AI always follows the rules.

Some rules in agent.md include: using short function names, avoiding magic numbers, always writing clear comments, and not changing unrelated code. There are also guidelines for clear commit messages and suggestions, like writing tests before fixing bugs. The author warns that these tips help, but you still need to review and test the code. AI can make mistakes, so human checks are still needed.

Another issue is “context dilution”—as the AI sees more information, it forgets instructions in the middle. To fix this, keep sessions short and reload agent.md when code quality drops. The author also suggests you can ask the AI to update agent.md for you, instead of editing by hand.

People in the comments had mixed views. Some liked the idea of agent.md, saying it’s smart to give AIs clear rules to follow. Others said that even with these files, AI code can still be wrong or low quality, so you must review everything. Some people shared their own agent.md files or offered extra rules they use. A few warned about context dilution, agreeing that you must restart sessions or reload instructions often. There were also comments about how AI tools make coding faster, but not always safer or better. Some users liked how agent.md made it easier to teach coding style to new team members, not just AIs. Others wish for better ways to keep AI tools following project rules, maybe built into IDEs. In general, most people agreed that clear instructions help, but AI is not a replacement for careful review.

## A complex structure on S^6 [pdf]

- 原文链接: [A complex structure on S^6 [pdf]](https://alpo.ge/s6.pdf)
- HN链接: [Hacker News讨论](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49412947)

This article claims that the six-dimensional sphere, S^6, can have a complex structure. This is a big deal because, for many years, mathematicians thought it was not possible.

The author builds a special three-dimensional complex manifold, called X, which is mapped onto the projective line (a kind of sphere). For most points, the fibers (the parts above each point) are 2-dimensional complex tori. At three special points, the fibers are more complicated: one is a special kind of surface with a hexagon shape, and the other two involve something called "logarithmic transforms." The author explains how these fibers are glued together using group actions and period functions, and shows that the whole space X is smooth and connected. The key point is that X has the same topology as S^6, meaning it is simply connected and its homology matches S^6. The author also computes many invariants, showing that X is not Kähler (a special type of complex manifold). Previous mathematical results, especially by Campana, Demailly, and Peternell (CDP), said that such a space should not exist. But the author carefully checks all the details, and finds that X is a counterexample to the earlier theory. The construction uses explicit matrices, special lattices, and group actions. The algebraic dimension of X is 1, meaning all its meromorphic functions come from the base sphere. The most technical part is how the special fibers are glued and how the monodromy (the way things twist around the special points) is handled.

In the comments, many people are surprised by the result. Some point out that, if true, this would solve a long-standing open problem in mathematics. Others are cautious, saying that the proof is long and technical, and it will take time for experts to check all the details. A few users discuss possible places where the construction might fail, or where earlier attempts went wrong. Some mathematicians explain that the key issue is whether the gluing at the special fiber (the hexagon) is really smooth and gives a true complex structure, not just a topological one. Others wonder if the contradiction with previous theorems is real, or if there is a subtle mistake. Several comments link to background papers about S^6 and complex structures, noting that this topic has a complicated history. A few people mention similar constructions in related areas, but agree that this one is new and surprising. In general, there is excitement, skepticism, and a sense that the math community will need time to fully understand and verify the proof.
